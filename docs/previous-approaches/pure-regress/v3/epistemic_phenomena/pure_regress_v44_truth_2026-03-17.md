# Recursive Causal Interrogation: Truth

## The Unconcealed

What does it mean for something to be unconcealed, and does the very act of unconcealing presuppose a prior state of concealment that itself requires explanation?
If truth is fundamentally an unconcealing, what is the nature of the concealment that precedes it, and can concealment exist without something that conceals?
Does the unconcealed present itself as unconcealed, or does recognition of unconcealedness require a further act of judgment that may itself distort what is revealed?
If unconcealing is a process, does the process have stages, and can truth be partially unconcealed, or is partial unconcealment itself a form of concealment?
Can the unconcealed ever be fully unconcealed, or does every revelation simultaneously create new dimensions of hiddenness that remain to be disclosed?
If complete unconcealment is impossible, does truth always carry within itself a residue of concealment, and what is the status of this irreducible remainder?
Does the unconcealed depend on someone to whom it is unconcealed, or can unconcealedness exist independently of any witness?
If truth requires a witness, does the witness's own concealed nature contaminate the unconcealing, such that what appears unconcealed is always filtered through opacity?
Is the distinction between concealment and unconcealment itself something that can be unconcealed, or does it operate as a precondition that can never fully appear?
What conceals the nature of concealment itself, and can this meta-concealment ever be penetrated without generating yet another layer?

---

Does truth as unconcealment differ from truth as correctness, and if so, which is more fundamental, or does asking which is more fundamental already presuppose one conception over the other?
If unconcealment is more originary than correctness, how did correctness come to dominate the understanding of truth, and what was concealed in that historical transition?
Can the history of truth's self-understanding be itself true, or does every historical account of truth already operate within a particular conception of truth that it cannot justify?
If the movement from unconcealment to correctness was itself a concealment, what would it mean to reverse that movement, and is reversal even possible from within the regime of correctness?
Does the unconcealed reveal being itself, or only beings, and what is the difference between the truth of being and the truth of particular entities?
If being itself can be unconcealed, to whom or what is it unconcealed, given that every witness is itself a being caught within being?
Can the nothing be unconcealed, and if truth is unconcealment, is there a truth of nothingness, or does nothingness resist all unconcealing?
Does the unconcealed withdraw in the very moment of its appearing, such that truth is always a play between presence and absence?
If truth is this play, can the play itself be made present, or does it necessarily withdraw behind every attempt to thematize it?

---

Is the clearing in which beings appear itself something unconcealed, or is it the condition for unconcealment that can never itself be fully illuminated?
What opens the clearing, and does the clearing have an origin, or is it always already there as the ungrounded ground of all appearing?
If the clearing is ungrounded, does truth ultimately rest on groundlessness, and what does it mean for truth to emerge from what has no foundation?
Can groundlessness itself be true, or does the concept of truth require some ground, however minimal, to have any meaning?
If truth requires ground and ground requires truth, is the relationship between them circular, and can circularity be the fundamental structure of truth?
Does circular truth differ from no truth at all, or is circularity itself a form of concealment that disguises the absence of genuine unconcealment?
What would it mean for truth to be genuinely without ground — would this be the deepest truth or the final dissolution of truth?
If the dissolution of truth is itself true, does truth survive its own dissolution, and what kind of truth would this survival constitute?
Can truth die, and if it can, what would persist in its absence — mere appearance, or something beyond the distinction between truth and appearance?
Is the question of whether truth can die itself a truthful question, or does it already assume what it interrogates?

---

Does unconcealment happen all at once or gradually, and does the temporality of unconcealing affect the nature of what is unconcealed?
If truth unfolds in time, is there a time before truth, and what would existence without truth look like?
Can we even conceive of a time before truth without already employing truth to do so?
Is truth eternal or historical, and if historical, does it change, and if it changes, was the previous truth false or merely a different mode of unconcealment?
If different epochs have different truths, is the claim that truth is epochal itself an eternal truth or merely the truth of our epoch?

---

Does the unconcealed have a structure, and if so, is this structure given by the unconcealed itself or imposed by the one to whom it is unconcealed?
If the structure is imposed, does every unconcealment involve a structuring that is also a distortion, and is undistorted unconcealment possible?
Can the unconcealed resist the structure imposed on it, and if so, how does this resistance manifest — as anomaly, as paradox, as the sense that something does not fit?
If the unconcealed resists structuring, does truth always contain an element of resistance — a surplus that exceeds every framework?
Is the surplus of the unconcealed what drives further inquiry, and does inquiry feed on the excess of truth over every particular disclosure?
Does language structure the unconcealed, and if so, does language enable or constrain unconcealment?
If language both enables and constrains, is every linguistic truth both a revelation and a limitation?
Can the unconcealed be encountered in its raw state, prior to all structuring, and what would this pre-structural encounter be like?
If the pre-structural encounter is impossible, is all truth always already structured, and is the dream of unmediated truth a philosophical illusion?
Does the multiplicity of languages suggest a multiplicity of unconcealments, and if so, does each language open a different clearing for truth?

---

Is the unconcealed the same as the manifest, and if not, what distinguishes unconcealment from mere manifestation?
Does manifestation require an audience while unconcealment does not, or is the distinction more subtle?
Can something be unconcealed to no one, existing in a state of open disclosure without any witness to receive it?
If witness-less unconcealment is possible, is truth fundamentally independent of awareness, persisting in the open even when no one is there?
Does the concept of unconcealment apply to the self — can the self be unconcealed to itself — or is self-unconcealment a special case with its own difficulties?
If self-unconcealment is possible, is it complete, or does the self always retain hidden depths even from itself?
Can one be unconcealed to others while remaining concealed from oneself, and is this a common human condition?
If unconcealment to others exceeds self-unconcealment, do others know us better than we know ourselves, and is the truth of the self located in the eyes of others?

## What Truth Is

What is truth, and does the question "what is truth" already presuppose an understanding of truth that determines in advance what kind of answer can be given?
If we need truth to ask about truth, is the inquiry into the nature of truth inescapably circular, and does this circularity invalidate or constitute the inquiry?
Can truth be defined, or does every definition of truth employ truth in a way that renders the definition circular?
If truth cannot be defined without circularity, does this mean truth is indefinable, and if indefinable, how can we speak about it at all?
Does truth have a nature, or is "truth" merely a word we apply to heterogeneous phenomena that share no common essence?
If truth has no essence, what unifies the various things we call true, and if nothing unifies them, why do we use a single word?
Is truth a property that belongs to things, or a relation between things, or a way things appear, or something else entirely?
If truth is a property, what kind of property is it — natural, logical, normative, or sui generis — and how does this property attach to its bearers?
Can the question "what is truth" be answered truly, and if so, does the answer's truth depend on the answer itself, creating a strange loop?
If the answer to "what is truth" must be true to be an answer, does truth validate itself, and is self-validation legitimate or viciously circular?

---

Is truth one thing or many things, and if many, are the different kinds of truth related, opposed, or simply different?
If there are multiple truths, can they conflict, and if conflicting truths are possible, does this undermine the very concept of truth?
Does the unity of truth require that there be one supreme truth from which all others derive, and what would this supreme truth be?
If there is a supreme truth, can it be known, and if known, does knowing it change what it is?
Is truth discovered or invented, and does the answer to this question depend on what truth is, creating another circularity?
If truth is discovered, it must exist prior to discovery — but where does it exist, and in what mode?
If truth is invented, what constrains the invention, and can truth be invented arbitrarily, or does something resist arbitrary invention?
What resists arbitrary invention of truth — is it reality, logic, other truths, or something we cannot name?
Can truth exist without anyone knowing it, and if so, what is the mode of existence of unknown truths?
If unknown truths exist, how do we know they exist, and does our knowing that unknown truths exist make them known, dissolving the category?

---

Is truth simple or complex, and if complex, what are its components, and how do they relate?
If truth is simple, how can it have the richness and variety it seems to have, and is apparent complexity an illusion?
If truth is complex, can its components exist independently of truth, or do they only become what they are within the whole?
Does truth have an opposite, and if so, is the opposite falsity, illusion, concealment, or something else?
If truth and its opposite are genuinely opposed, do they share something in common that makes opposition possible, and what is this common ground?
Can truth and falsity coexist in the same bearer at the same time and in the same respect, and what does the answer reveal about the nature of truth?
Is the distinction between truth and falsity exhaustive, or is there a third possibility — neither true nor false — and what would this territory look like?
If there is a realm beyond truth and falsity, is it more fundamental than either, and does truth emerge from this more basic domain?
Does truth have degrees, or is it all-or-nothing, and what are the consequences of each view for our understanding of knowledge and inquiry?

---

Is truth normative — does it make demands on us — or is it merely descriptive, a feature of things that carries no obligation?
If truth is normative, what is the source of its authority, and why should we care about truth rather than useful falsehood?
Can truth compel, or can it only invite, and what kind of force does truth exert on those who encounter it?
If truth compels, does it thereby constrain freedom, and is the compulsion of truth a form of oppression or liberation?
If truth only invites, can the invitation be declined, and what is the status of one who knowingly turns away from truth?

---

Does truth have a history — has the meaning of truth changed over time — and if so, is the history of truth itself true?
If the history of truth is true, which concept of truth validates the history — the current one, the earliest one, or a meta-concept that encompasses all historical variants?
Can truth be understood independently of its history, or is truth always embedded in a historical context that shapes its meaning?
If truth is historically embedded, does stepping outside history give access to ahistorical truth, and is such stepping-outside possible?
Has truth always been important, or was there a time when truth did not matter, and what would a truth-indifferent culture look like?
If truth-indifference is possible, does this show that the value of truth is contingent rather than necessary?
Can truth be outgrown — can humanity reach a stage where truth is no longer the relevant category — and what would replace it?
If truth can be outgrown, is the concept of truth merely a phase in the development of thought, and is there a post-truth that is not merely anti-truth?
Does the etymology of "truth" in various languages reveal different original understandings, and do these differences matter philosophically?
If different languages encode different original understandings of truth, does translation between truth-concepts preserve or distort what each tradition means by truth?

---

Is truth a human concept, or would non-human intelligences also develop the concept of truth?
If truth is uniquely human, does this make truth parochial — a feature of one species' way of coping with reality?
If truth is universal to all intelligences, does this suggest that truth is a feature of reality itself rather than a feature of minds?
Can truth exist without minds, and if so, is truth a property of the universe prior to the emergence of consciousness?
If truth is a property of the universe, is the universe itself true, and what would it mean for the universe to be true?
Does the concept of truth apply to fictional worlds, and are there truths within fiction that are as robust as truths within reality?
If fictional truths exist, does the concept of truth extend beyond actuality to possibility, and is possible truth genuine truth?
Can paradoxical truths exist — truths that are both true and not true — and does their existence expand or destroy the concept of truth?

## Truth and Correspondence

Does truth consist in correspondence between thought and reality, and if so, what is the nature of this correspondence?
What corresponds to what in the correspondence theory — do propositions correspond to facts, beliefs to states of affairs, or sentences to situations?
How can something mental or linguistic correspond to something non-mental and non-linguistic, given that correspondence seems to require similarity, and thought is nothing like a rock?
If correspondence does not require similarity, what does it require, and how is the correspondence relation itself established?
Can the correspondence relation be verified, and if so, how do we step outside both thought and reality to compare them?
If we cannot step outside thought to compare it with reality, is the correspondence theory self-defeating, asserting something it can never confirm?
Does the correspondence theory presuppose a reality independent of thought, and can this presupposition itself be verified without circularity?
If reality is independent of thought, how does thought ever make contact with it, and what mediates between the mental and the real?
Is the correspondence theory itself true by correspondence, and if so, what fact does it correspond to?
If the correspondence theory corresponds to a fact about truth, how is this meta-correspondence established without an infinite regress of correspondences?

---

What is a fact, and do facts exist independently of the propositions that state them, or are facts simply true propositions viewed from the side of the world?
If facts are independent of propositions, how are they individuated — what makes one fact distinct from another?
If facts are not independent of propositions, does the correspondence theory collapse into a circular definition where truth is correspondence with what is true?
Can there be facts that no proposition captures, and if so, are there truths that cannot be stated?
If there are unstatable truths, does the correspondence theory fail for them, and what theory of truth applies to truths beyond language?
Are negative facts real — does the fact that unicorns do not exist correspond to something in reality, and if so, what?
If negative facts exist, is reality far more populated than it appears, containing not only what is but also infinitely many absences?
If negative facts do not exist, how can negative propositions be true by correspondence, and must the correspondence theory be supplemented or abandoned?
What about counterfactual truths — what fact does "if I had dropped the glass, it would have broken" correspond to?
Do possible facts exist, and if truth is correspondence with facts, are there truths about mere possibilities?

---

Does the correspondence theory require a mirror-like relationship between the structure of language and the structure of reality?
If language must mirror reality, does this mean reality has a logical structure, and if so, is the logical structure discovered or imposed?
If the logical structure of reality is imposed by language, then correspondence is not between thought and an independent reality but between thought and a reality already shaped by thought — does this destroy the theory?
Can correspondence be partial, such that a proposition corresponds to reality in some respects but not others, and if so, is it partially true?
If partial truth is possible, does this undermine the correspondence theory's claim to provide a clear criterion of truth?
What counts as a successful correspondence — exact match, sufficient similarity, structural isomorphism, or something else?
If correspondence requires exact match, can any proposition achieve it, given the inevitable gap between representation and reality?
If approximate correspondence suffices, how approximate can it be while still counting as truth, and who decides the threshold?
Does the correspondence theory apply to all domains — to mathematical truths, moral truths, aesthetic truths — or only to empirical claims about the physical world?
If correspondence applies only to empirical claims, what theory of truth governs the rest, and are there multiple fundamentally different kinds of truth?

---

Does the correspondence theory assume a static reality that propositions mirror, and if reality is dynamic, can static propositions correspond to it?
If reality is dynamic and propositions are static, does every true proposition become false as reality changes, and is truth always already outdated?
Can the correspondence theory accommodate truths about abstract objects — numbers, sets, properties — that do not seem to inhabit the spatiotemporal world?
If abstract truths exist, what do they correspond to, and is the correspondence theory committed to a realm of abstract entities?
Does the correspondence theory require that reality have a determinate structure, and if reality is indeterminate (as quantum mechanics suggests), can propositions correspond to indeterminate states?
If correspondence with indeterminate states is possible, is truth itself indeterminate, and does indeterminate truth make sense?
Can the correspondence theory explain why truth matters — why does corresponding to reality have value, and is correspondence valuable in itself or only instrumentally?
If correspondence is instrumentally valuable, does the correspondence theory reduce to pragmatism — truth matters because it works?
Is the correspondence theory a theory of truth or a theory of meaning — does it tell us what truth is or what true sentences mean?
If the correspondence theory is about meaning rather than truth, does it leave the nature of truth itself unexplained?

---

Can correspondence be a matter of degree — can a proposition correspond more or less well to reality — and if so, is truth a continuum rather than a binary?
If truth is a continuum, where on the continuum does something become true enough to count as true?
Does the correspondence theory work for self-referential truths — what does "this sentence is true" correspond to?
If self-referential truths lack clear correspondents, does the correspondence theory fail for a significant class of truths?
Can the correspondence theory account for necessary truths — truths that could not have been otherwise — and what do necessary truths correspond to in all possible worlds?
If necessary truths correspond to features of all possible worlds, does this require the existence of possible worlds, and is this ontological commitment acceptable?
Does the correspondence theory's commitment to an independent reality make it incompatible with idealism, and is idealism therefore committed to a different theory of truth?
If idealism rejects correspondence, can idealism account for error — how can an idea be false if reality is ideas?
Is the correspondence theory the common-sense view of truth, and if so, does common sense carry philosophical weight?
If common sense supports correspondence, can common sense be philosophically wrong, and should we trust philosophical sophistication over common sense?

## Truth and Coherence

Does truth consist in the coherence of beliefs with one another, and if so, what kind of coherence — logical consistency, mutual support, explanatory integration?
If truth is coherence, can a perfectly coherent set of beliefs be entirely false, describing a world that does not exist?
Does the possibility of coherent but false belief systems refute the coherence theory, or does it merely show that coherence is necessary but not sufficient for truth?
If coherence is necessary but not sufficient, what else is needed, and does the addition of this extra element make coherence secondary?
Can coherence be measured, and if so, is there a unique maximally coherent system, or can there be multiple equally coherent but mutually incompatible systems?
If multiple equally coherent systems exist, and truth is coherence, are all of them true, and does this lead to relativism?
If the coherence theory leads to relativism, is this a reductio of the theory, or does it reveal something genuine about the nature of truth?
How does a new belief get evaluated for coherence — against the existing system, or against the ideally coherent system — and how is the ideal system known?
If the ideally coherent system is never fully known, does the coherence theory provide a criterion of truth that is in principle inapplicable?
Does the coherence theory apply to itself — is it true because it coheres with other beliefs about truth, and is this self-application legitimate?

---

What counts as coherence — is it merely the absence of contradiction, or does it require positive explanatory connections between beliefs?
If coherence requires only consistency, then any set of unrelated but non-contradictory beliefs would be coherent — is this too weak a criterion?
If coherence requires explanatory connections, who determines what counts as an explanatory connection, and does this introduce subjective judgment into truth?
Can coherence exist among false beliefs, and if so, does coherence guarantee nothing about the relationship between beliefs and reality?
If coherence guarantees nothing about reality, why should anyone value coherence, and is the pursuit of coherence merely an aesthetic preference?
Does the coherence theory presuppose that reality itself is coherent, and if reality were chaotic, would the theory fail?
How do we know whether reality is coherent — by checking our coherent beliefs against reality, which would require the correspondence theory?
If the coherence theory needs correspondence to validate the coherence of reality, is it parasitic on the very theory it claims to replace?
Can the coherence theory account for perceptual truth — is "the cat is on the mat" true because it coheres with other beliefs, or because of a direct encounter with a cat?
If perceptual truth requires something beyond coherence, does the coherence theory fail for the most basic truths we possess?

---

Does coherence extend across time, such that beliefs must cohere not only with present beliefs but with past and future beliefs?
If temporal coherence is required, how far back and forward must coherence extend, and does any belief system achieve this?
Can a single anomalous experience justify revising an otherwise coherent system, or must coherence always win over isolated experience?
If coherence always wins, does the theory make belief systems immune to correction by reality, and is this a virtue or a vice?
If isolated experience can override coherence, then coherence is not the ultimate criterion, and what determines when experience should prevail?
Is the coherence theory a theory of truth or merely a theory of justified belief, and does this distinction matter?
If coherence is about justification rather than truth, can beliefs be justified but false, and what good is justification without truth?
Does the coherence theory eliminate the gap between how things seem and how things are, or does it merely redefine truth to mean how things seem from within a coherent system?
If truth becomes how things seem from within a coherent system, has truth lost its critical function — its ability to challenge what seems to be the case?
Without a gap between appearance and truth, is the very concept of inquiry undermined, since inquiry presupposes that things might not be as they appear?

---

Does coherence presuppose a system, and if so, who or what determines the boundaries of the system within which coherence is assessed?
If the system's boundaries are arbitrary, does the coherence theory yield arbitrary truths depending on how the system is defined?
Can two mutually exclusive systems both be internally coherent, and if so, does the coherence theory force us to accept both as true?
If both are true, and they contradict each other, has the coherence theory generated a contradiction, and is this a reductio of the theory?
Does the coherence theory work better for some domains — mathematics, logic, ethics — than for others, and is it a partial rather than universal theory of truth?
If coherence is a partial theory, what supplements it, and is truth fundamentally pluralistic — requiring different theories for different domains?
Can a belief cohere with a system while being based on a misunderstanding, and if so, does coherence fail to distinguish genuine from spurious integration?
If coherence cannot distinguish genuine from spurious integration, does it lack the resources to identify error within a coherent system?
Is the most coherent system the one that explains the most with the fewest assumptions, and if so, does coherence reduce to explanatory power?
If coherence reduces to explanatory power, is truth just explanatory success, and does explanatory success guarantee truth?

---

Does the coherence theory make truth conservative — favoring beliefs that fit the existing system over novel beliefs that challenge it?
If the coherence theory is conservative, does it resist revolutionary truths that overturn existing frameworks?
Can the coherence theory accommodate paradigm shifts, and if not, does it fail as an account of scientific truth?
If paradigm shifts violate coherence, is the period during a paradigm shift a period without truth?
Does the coherence theory require that there be a single best system, and if multiple equally coherent systems exist, is there a criterion for choosing?
If there is no criterion for choosing among equally coherent systems, is truth underdetermined by coherence?
Can coherence be formalized, and if so, does the formalization capture the intuitive notion of coherence, or does it lose something essential?
If formalization loses something, is coherence an inherently informal notion, and can informal notions ground a rigorous theory of truth?
Does the coherence theory assume that reality is rational — that reality hangs together coherently — and is this assumption justified?
If reality is not rational, can truth be coherent, and does an irrational reality defeat the coherence theory?

## Truth and Pragmatism

Is truth simply what works, and if so, what counts as working — practical success, predictive accuracy, problem-solving efficacy, or something else?
If truth is what works, does this mean that false beliefs that happen to work are true, and true beliefs that fail to work are false?
Can the same belief work in one context and fail in another, and if so, is it true in one context and false in another?
If truth is context-dependent, is the claim that truth is context-dependent itself context-dependent, or is it universally true?
Does pragmatism reduce truth to utility, and if so, is utility itself defined independently of truth, or does defining utility require truth?
If utility requires truth for its definition, does the pragmatic theory presuppose what it defines, making it circular?
Can the pragmatic theory account for truths that have no practical consequences — pure mathematical truths, historical truths about forgotten events, truths about the very distant past?
If some truths have no practical consequences, does pragmatism fail for them, and what theory of truth applies to the impractical true?
Does pragmatism confuse the criteria by which we identify truth with the nature of truth itself?
Can a theory be useful without being true, and if so, doesn't this show that usefulness and truth are different things?

---

If truth is what works in the long run, how long is the long run, and who lives long enough to verify it?
Does "working in the long run" mean working for all time, and if so, is pragmatic truth an ideal that can never be confirmed?
If pragmatic truth can never be confirmed, how does it differ from correspondence truth, which also seems to be an ideal beyond verification?
Can pragmatism account for unpleasant truths — truths that work against our interests, that are practically harmful to acknowledge?
If an unpleasant truth works against our interests, does pragmatism say it is false, and does this make pragmatism a theory of wishful thinking?
Does pragmatism make truth anthropocentric — true for us — and if so, is there no truth for beings with different practical needs?
If different beings have different practical needs, and truth is what works, do different beings inhabit different truths?
If multiple species-relative truths exist, is there a truth that transcends all practical perspectives, and if so, how would any practical being access it?
Can the pragmatic theory account for the truth of its own claims — is it true because it works, and does its working validate it?
If pragmatism is true because it works, what happens when it stops working — does it become false, and was it ever really true?

---

Does the pragmatic theory eliminate the distinction between truth and power, since what works often depends on who has the power to make things work?
If truth is what works and power determines what works, is truth merely a function of power, and does this make truth political?
Can the powerless possess truth, or does pragmatism imply that truth belongs to those with the resources to make beliefs effective?
If truth belongs to the powerful, is the claim that truth belongs to the powerful itself a product of power rather than truth?
Does pragmatism make truth contingent on human practices, and if practices change, does truth change with them?
If truth changes with practice, are past truths retroactively falsified by new practices, or do they remain true for their time?
Can pragmatism distinguish between beliefs that genuinely work and beliefs we merely think work, and does this distinction require a non-pragmatic concept of truth?
If we need non-pragmatic truth to distinguish real from apparent working, has pragmatism defeated itself?
What is the pragmatic cash value of the concept of truth itself — what practical difference does having the concept of truth make?
If the concept of truth makes no practical difference, does pragmatism imply that truth is meaningless, and is this conclusion self-refuting?

---

Does pragmatism conflate the order of discovery with the order of being — confusing how we find truth with what truth is?
If how we find truth differs from what truth is, is pragmatism an epistemological theory masquerading as a metaphysical one?
Can pragmatism account for truths about the past, where the practical consequences have already unfolded and cannot be tested?
If truths about the past resist pragmatic testing, is pragmatism limited to forward-looking truths, and is truth about the past a different kind?
Does pragmatism's emphasis on consequences make it unable to account for mathematical truths, which have no consequences beyond mathematics?
If mathematical truths have no practical consequences, are they true in a non-pragmatic sense, and does this show pragmatism's limits?
Can different people find different beliefs practical, and if so, does pragmatism lead to individual relativism about truth?
If pragmatism leads to relativism, can it maintain the distinction between what is true and what merely seems useful, and is this distinction essential?
Does pragmatism explain why some false beliefs work — is the pragmatist committed to calling working falsehoods true?
If working falsehoods are called true by pragmatism, has pragmatism abandoned the ordinary concept of truth in favor of a technical redefinition?

---

Does the pragmatic theory apply to itself — is pragmatism true because it works — and if it stops working, does it become false?
If pragmatism becomes false when it stops working, was it ever genuinely true, or was it only temporarily useful?
Can pragmatism account for tragic truths — truths that do not work for anyone, that serve no practical purpose, that merely describe suffering?
If tragic truths resist pragmatic analysis, does pragmatism fail for the truths that matter most existentially?
Does pragmatism privilege action over contemplation, and if so, does it devalue contemplative truths — truths known in stillness?
If contemplative truths are devalued, is pragmatism hostile to philosophy, mysticism, and any form of knowledge that does not lead to action?
Can pragmatism distinguish between short-term and long-term utility, and does this distinction affect which beliefs count as true?
If short-term and long-term utility conflict, which determines truth, and is the pragmatist forced to make a non-pragmatic choice?
Does pragmatism's focus on consequences make it consequentialist about truth, and is consequentialism about truth as problematic as consequentialism about ethics?
If consequentialism about truth is problematic, does this show that truth has intrinsic value beyond its consequences?

## Truth and Consensus

Does truth consist in what everyone would agree upon under ideal conditions, and if so, what are ideal conditions, and who defines them?
If ideal conditions are defined as conditions that produce truth, is the consensus theory circular, defining truth through conditions that presuppose truth?
Can consensus be wrong, and if so, doesn't this show that truth is independent of consensus?
If consensus can be wrong, what makes it wrong — correspondence with reality, coherence with other truths, or something else — and does this make consensus derivative?
Does the consensus theory confuse the social processes by which truth is recognized with the nature of truth itself?
If truth is prior to consensus, why does consensus matter at all, and is it merely an imperfect indicator of pre-existing truth?
Can truth exist in the absence of any community capable of consensus, and if so, is truth fundamentally non-social?
If truth is non-social, how do we explain the apparently social character of knowledge, inquiry, and the resolution of disagreement?
Does the ideal speech situation — free from coercion, distortion, and unequal power — guarantee truth, or could ideal speakers agree on falsehoods?
If ideal speakers could agree on falsehoods, does the consensus theory fail, and what would count as a genuine guarantee of truth?

---

Who is included in the relevant consensus — all humans, all rational beings, all possible minds, or some other group?
If the consensus must include all possible minds, is this an achievable standard, and can a theory of truth rest on an unachievable condition?
Does the consensus theory privilege rational agreement over other modes of access to truth, such as intuition, perception, or mystical experience?
If rational agreement is privileged, what justifies this privilege, and is the justification itself a product of rational agreement?
Can consensus change over time, and if so, does truth change with it, or does changing consensus merely reflect our changing relationship to unchanging truth?
If consensus is stable over time, does stability guarantee truth, or could a persistent consensus be persistently wrong?
How does the consensus theory handle deep, irresolvable disagreements — are the matters under dispute truthless, or is there a truth that consensus cannot reach?
If there are truths beyond the reach of consensus, does the consensus theory fail precisely where truth matters most?
Does the consensus theory make truth democratic, and if so, is this a virtue or a trivialization?
Can a single individual possess truth against the consensus of all others, and if so, how would this truth ever be recognized?

---

Does consensus require communication, and if so, is truth limited to what can be communicated?
If truth is limited to what can be communicated, are there incommunicable truths, and how do they relate to the consensus theory?
Does the medium of communication affect the consensus, and if so, do different media produce different truths?
