# Recursive Causal Interrogation: Morality

## The Ought

What is it to say that something ought to be the case?

---

Does "ought" describe something about the world, or does it prescribe something to the world that the world does not yet contain?

If "ought" prescribes, who or what is doing the prescribing, and to whom or what is it addressed?

Can an "ought" exist without a subject who recognizes it, or does recognition constitute the "ought" as such?

If no one recognizes an obligation, does the obligation persist, or does it vanish like a sound in an empty room?

Does the persistence of obligation independent of recognition require a metaphysical ground — a moral reality — or can obligation be real without being grounded in anything beyond itself?

If obligation grounds itself, is that self-grounding circular, or is it a different logical structure altogether?

Can something be its own ground without collapsing into incoherence?

Does the demand for a ground of the ground generate an infinite regress, and if so, does this regress undermine morality or reveal something essential about its structure?

If morality is essentially ungrounded, does that make it weaker or stronger than things that rest on foundations?

Is the foundationless character of morality its fragility or its freedom?

---

Where does the "ought" come from — from reason, from feeling, from God, from nature, from convention, or from somewhere that precedes all of these?

If the "ought" comes from reason, can reason alone motivate action, or does it require a prior desire to be rational?

If reason requires desire in order to motivate, is the "ought" ultimately rooted in desire rather than reason?

Can desire itself generate an "ought," or does desire merely indicate what one wants without saying anything about what one should want?

Is there a difference between what one wants and what one should want, and if so, what is the source of that difference?

Does the difference between want and should presuppose a standard external to desire, and if so, what is that standard?

If the standard is reason, we return to the question of whether reason can be practical — does this circle have an exit?

Could the "ought" be primitive — not derivable from anything else — and if so, does its primitiveness make it mysterious or simply fundamental?

Is everything fundamental also mysterious, or are some things so basic that asking for their explanation is a category error?

---

Can "ought" be derived from "is," or is Hume's guillotine absolute?

If no amount of factual description yields a normative conclusion, where does normativity enter the picture?

Does normativity enter through human subjectivity, or does it exist independently of any subject?

If normativity is subjective, can it bind anyone who does not feel bound by it?

If normativity is objective, how does an objective norm make contact with a subjective agent?

Is the problem of how norms motivate action the same as the problem of how mind connects to body?

Does the mind-body problem recur within ethics as the is-ought problem?

Are all philosophical problems ultimately variations of one problem, or does each domain generate genuinely distinct difficulties?

If the is-ought problem is genuinely distinct, what makes normative questions different in kind from descriptive questions?

Is the distinction between normative and descriptive itself normative or descriptive?

---

Is the "ought" experienced as a demand, a pull, an invitation, or a constraint?

Does the phenomenology of moral experience differ from person to person, or is there a universal structure to the experience of being morally addressed?

If moral experience has a universal structure, does that universality prove anything about morality's objective status?

Can a universal experience be universally mistaken?

If moral experience is an illusion, what is doing the illuding, and why would evolution produce such a persistent illusion?

Does the evolutionary utility of moral experience count as evidence for or against its truth?

Can something be both useful and true, and if so, does utility provide any evidence of truth?

Or is the relationship between utility and truth entirely contingent?

If moral beliefs are useful regardless of their truth, does this make morality more like a tool than a discovery?

Can a tool also be a discovery — can we discover something by inventing it?

## What Morality Is

Can morality be defined, or does every definition of morality already presuppose a moral standpoint?

---

Is morality a set of rules, a set of virtues, a set of values, a way of seeing, a way of feeling, or something that resists categorization altogether?

If morality is a set of rules, where do the rules come from, and what gives them authority?

If the authority of moral rules derives from their content, then the content must be evaluated by some standard — what is that standard?

If the standard is itself a moral rule, the justification is circular — does this circle vitiate morality or constitute it?

Can a system be self-justifying without being arbitrary?

Is self-justification always circular, or can a system justify itself through its coherence, its explanatory power, or its practical success?

Does practical success count as justification in ethics, or does success merely show that a system works without showing that it is right?

Can something work without being right, and can something be right without working?

If a moral system is right but unworkable, is it genuinely right, or does "right" in ethics necessarily include "livable"?

Does the demand that morality be livable smuggle in a consequentialist assumption — that morality must produce good outcomes for those who practice it?

---

Is morality one thing or many things — is there a single phenomenon called "morality" or a family of loosely related practices that we group together under one name?

If morality is a family resemblance concept, what holds the family together?

Does the unity of morality depend on a single source (God, reason, nature), or can morality be unified without a single source?

If morality has multiple sources, can these sources conflict, and if they conflict, what adjudicates between them?

Is the need for adjudication itself a moral need, and if so, does morality presuppose itself?

Can morality presuppose itself without being viciously circular?

Is there a difference between a vicious circle and a hermeneutic circle, and does morality exhibit the latter rather than the former?

If morality is a hermeneutic circle — understood only from within — can it be criticized from without?

Does the impossibility of an external standpoint on morality mean that all moral criticism is internal criticism?

Can internal criticism be genuinely radical, or is it always limited by the system it criticizes from within?

---

Is morality essentially human, or could there be moral agents who are not human?

If morality is essentially human, does it perish with humanity?

If morality is not essentially human, what is the minimal condition for moral agency?

Is rationality the minimal condition, or can a being be a moral agent without being rational?

Can a being that acts from instinct alone be a moral agent, and if not, what must be added to instinct to produce morality?

Is morality what happens when instinct becomes reflective, and is reflection sufficient for morality?

Can one reflect on one's instincts and still act immorally, and if so, what does this say about the relationship between reflection and morality?

Does morality require not just reflection but a particular kind of reflection — and if so, what kind?

Is the relevant kind of reflection impartial reflection, empathetic reflection, or something else altogether?

Can the kind of reflection morality requires itself be specified without already assuming a moral framework?

---

Does morality have a history, and if so, does its historicity undermine or enrich it?

If morality changes over time, is there moral progress, or merely moral change?

What would moral progress require — an objective standard against which change could be measured as improvement?

If there is an objective standard, does it exist outside history, and if so, how does a historical being access it?

If the standard exists within history, does it change along with everything else, making "progress" a moving target?

Can a moving target still be a genuine target, or does the motion of the standard dissolve the concept of progress?

Is moral progress an illusion that each generation constructs to flatter itself?

If moral progress is an illusion, how do we account for the abolition of slavery, the extension of rights, the prohibition of torture — are these not improvements?

Can something look like progress from one perspective and regress from another?

Does the existence of multiple perspectives make progress perspectival, or is there a perspective from which the judgment of progress is more accurate?

## Morality and Nature

Is morality natural or artificial — does it grow from nature or stand against it?

---

If morality is natural, does it follow that whatever is natural is moral?

Does the claim that morality is natural commit the naturalistic fallacy, or does it avoid the fallacy by not equating the natural with the good?

Can morality be rooted in nature without everything natural being thereby moral?

If some natural things are moral and others are not, what distinguishes moral nature from immoral nature?

Is the distinction between moral and immoral nature itself a moral distinction, imposed on nature from without?

If the distinction is imposed from without, who imposes it, and what gives them the authority to carve nature into moral and immoral categories?

Does nature itself contain moral distinctions — is there a moral structure built into reality — or is all moral structure projected by moral agents?

If moral structure is projected, is projection arbitrary, or does it follow some logic?

What is the logic of moral projection, and is that logic itself natural or artificial?

If the logic of moral projection is natural, does that make the projected morality natural after all, in a second-order sense?

---

Do animals exhibit morality, or do they merely exhibit behavior that resembles morality without being moral?

What would have to be true of an animal's behavior for it to count as genuinely moral rather than merely proto-moral?

Is the distinction between moral and proto-moral behavior a matter of degree or a matter of kind?

If it is a matter of degree, is there a threshold, and if so, what determines where the threshold falls?

If it is a matter of kind, what is the kind-making difference — language, self-consciousness, the ability to act against inclination?

Can an animal act against its inclination, and if so, does that already constitute a rudimentary morality?

If acting against inclination is necessary for morality, does this mean that morality is essentially a matter of overcoming oneself?

Is self-overcoming the essence of morality, or is this a specifically ascetic and perhaps distorted picture?

Can morality be joyful rather than effortful, and if so, does joyful morality count as morality at all?

Does the association of morality with effort and sacrifice reveal something essential about morality, or does it reveal a cultural bias?

---

Does evolution explain morality, or does it explain morality away?

If morality evolved because it enhanced survival, does this genetic explanation undermine the authority of moral claims?

Can something have an evolutionary explanation and still be objectively true — does the origin of a belief determine its validity?

If origins do not determine validity, then the evolutionary origin of morality is irrelevant to its truth — but is this too quick?

Does the specific way morality evolved — through kin selection, reciprocal altruism, group selection — constrain what morality can be?

If morality is an adaptation for group living, does it apply only within groups, and does it have anything to say about strangers?

Is the extension of morality beyond the in-group a natural development or a cultural achievement?

If extending morality beyond the group goes against evolutionary programming, does that mean universalist morality is unnatural?

Can the unnatural be moral, and if so, does morality sometimes require us to transcend nature?

If morality requires transcending nature, what is doing the transcending — is it something supernatural, or is transcendence itself a natural capacity?

---

Is the body morally relevant, or is morality purely a matter of mind?

If the body is morally relevant, is it relevant as a source of moral knowledge, as a site of moral concern, or as both?

Does the body know things the mind does not — are there moral truths accessible only through embodied experience?

If moral knowledge is partly bodily, does this mean that beings with different bodies have different moral knowledge?

Does the moral knowledge of a body in pain differ from the moral knowledge of a body at ease?

Is pain a moral teacher, and if so, what does it teach?

Does pain teach that suffering is bad, or does it teach something more complex — about vulnerability, dependence, and the need for care?

If pain teaches the need for care, does morality originate in the experience of neediness?

Is neediness the foundation of morality — is morality what happens when needful beings encounter each other?

Can beings that need nothing be moral, and if not, does morality presuppose finitude?

## Morality and Culture

Is morality a cultural product, and if so, is it merely a cultural product?

---

If morality varies across cultures, does this variation prove that morality is relative, or only that moral knowledge is imperfect?

Can moral disagreement between cultures be genuine disagreement — disagreement about the same thing — or do different cultures mean different things by "morality"?

If different cultures mean different things by morality, is there a cross-cultural concept of morality, or only a family of culturally specific concepts?

If there is no cross-cultural concept of morality, can different cultures communicate about moral matters at all?

Does moral communication require shared moral concepts, or can it proceed through translation?

Is moral translation possible without distortion, or does every act of moral translation impose the categories of one culture on another?

If moral translation always distorts, does this mean that moral understanding across cultures is impossible?

Can moral understanding proceed through something other than translation — through shared experience, through empathy, through bodily co-presence?

Is empathy a cultural capacity or a biological one, and does its origin affect its reliability as a bridge between moral worlds?

If empathy can bridge moral worlds, does this suggest that there is something beneath cultural variation — a common moral ground?

---

Does culture create morality, or does morality create culture?

If culture creates morality, then before culture there was no morality — but could beings without morality create culture?

Does the creation of culture require cooperation, and does cooperation require morality?

If cooperation requires morality, and morality requires culture, then neither can precede the other — does this mean they co-emerged?

What does co-emergence look like, and does co-emergence dissolve the question of priority, or does it merely defer it?

Is the question of whether morality or culture came first a meaningful question, or does it rest on a false assumption that one must precede the other?

Can two things be genuinely simultaneous in their origin, or does temporal analysis always reveal a sequence?

If morality and culture are genuinely co-original, does this mean that morality is always already cultural and culture is always already moral?

If so, can we ever separate the moral from the cultural, and if we cannot, what happens to the project of moral criticism of culture?

Can culture criticize itself morally if morality is part of culture?

---

Is moral education a form of indoctrination, and if so, is all indoctrination wrong?

Can a child acquire morality without being indoctrinated, or does the very process of moral formation require shaping a child's responses before the child can consent to being shaped?

If moral education necessarily precedes the capacity to consent to it, does this make all moral education an imposition?

Can an imposition be legitimate, and if so, what makes a legitimate imposition different from an illegitimate one?

Is the difference between education and indoctrination a difference of method, of content, or of intent?

If the difference is one of intent, can well-intentioned indoctrination be legitimate?

Does the concept of indoctrination presuppose a distinction between true and false moral beliefs — that indoctrination instills false beliefs while education instills true ones?

If this is the distinction, does it not beg the question by assuming we already know which moral beliefs are true?

Can moral education be neutral between competing moral views, or is neutrality itself a moral position?

If neutrality is a moral position, does the project of neutral moral education undermine itself?

---

Do moral traditions have authority, and if so, what is the source of that authority?

Is a moral tradition authoritative because it has survived, because it has been tested by experience, or because it embodies accumulated wisdom?

Can a tradition that has survived for centuries be deeply wrong, and if so, does longevity count for nothing?

If longevity counts for something but not everything, how much does it count, and how do we weigh it against other considerations?

Is the authority of tradition conservative by nature — does respect for tradition always counsel against change?

Can tradition itself demand change — can a tradition contain within it the resources for its own transformation?

If a tradition can transform itself, is the transformed tradition the same tradition, or a new one wearing the old one's name?

Is continuity of tradition a matter of content, of practice, of identity, or of narrative?

Can a tradition that has changed all its content but maintained its narrative identity still claim the authority of the original?

Does the ship of Theseus problem apply to moral traditions, and if so, what does this imply about their authority?

## Morality and Religion

Does morality depend on God, or does God depend on morality?

---

If morality depends on God, is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?

If something is good because God commands it, then God could make torture good by commanding it — is this acceptable?

If God commands things because they are good, then goodness is independent of God, and God is not the source of morality — can a theist accept this?

Does the Euthyphro dilemma have a resolution, or does it permanently destabilize the relationship between religion and morality?

Could the dilemma be resolved by saying that God's nature is the good — that God does not choose the good but is the good?

If God's nature is the good, can God's nature be understood without already possessing a concept of goodness?

If we need a concept of goodness to understand God's nature, does goodness precede God conceptually, even if God precedes goodness ontologically?

Is the conceptual order the same as the ontological order, and if not, which order matters more for understanding morality?

Can conceptual priority and ontological priority come apart, and what happens to moral theory when they do?

Does the distinction between conceptual and ontological priority itself have moral implications?

---

Can there be morality without God, or does the death of God entail the death of morality?

If God is dead, is everything permitted, as Dostoevsky suggested, or does morality survive the death of God?

What would morality without God look like — would it be the same morality with a different justification, or a fundamentally different morality?

If morality without God is fundamentally different, does this confirm the theist's claim that morality depends on God?

Can secular morality generate the same motivational force as religious morality, or does the absence of divine reward and punishment weaken moral motivation?

If secular morality is motivationally weaker, does this show that religious morality is true, or only that it is more effective?

Is effectiveness a virtue in a moral system, and if so, is it the most important virtue?

Can a true moral system be ineffective, and can an effective moral system be false?

If truth and effectiveness can come apart in morality, what should we prefer — a true morality that no one follows or a false morality that everyone follows?

Is this dilemma real, or does it rest on the assumption that truth and effectiveness are independent — which they may not be?

---

Does religious experience provide moral knowledge — does encountering the sacred teach one how to live?

If religious experience provides moral knowledge, is this knowledge available only to those who have the experience, or can it be communicated?

If it can only be known through experience, is religious moral knowledge a form of private knowledge, and can private knowledge be authoritative for others?

If it can be communicated, does the communication translate the experience or merely describe it?

Is moral knowledge gained through religious experience different in kind from moral knowledge gained through reason, or are they the same knowledge reached by different routes?

If they are different in kind, can they conflict, and if they conflict, which takes precedence?

Does reason have authority over revelation, or does revelation have authority over reason?

Can reason and revelation be harmonized, or is every harmonization a distortion of one or both?

If every harmonization is a distortion, must the moral agent choose between reason and revelation?

Is the choice itself a moral choice, and if so, by what standard is it made — by reason, by revelation, or by something else?

---

Is the concept of sin a moral concept, and if so, what does it add to the concept of wrongdoing?

Does sin name something more than moral transgression — does it name a rupture in the relationship between the human and the divine?

If sin is relational, does it presuppose the existence of a divine being to whom one is related?

Can the concept of sin be secularized — can there be sin without God?

If sin without God is incoherent, does the loss of God entail the loss of a moral concept that secular morality cannot replace?

What, if anything, does secular morality lose when it abandons the concept of sin?

Is guilt a secular substitute for sin, and if so, is it an adequate substitute?

Does guilt capture everything sin captures, or does sin contain a dimension — the dimension of the sacred — that guilt cannot reach?

If the sacred is a genuine dimension of moral experience, can morality be complete without it?

Can morality be incomplete, and if so, what would a complete morality look like?

## Morality and Reason

Is morality rational, and if so, in what sense of "rational"?

---

Does reason discover morality, or does it construct morality?

If reason discovers morality, where does morality exist prior to its discovery?

Is there a realm of moral facts, analogous to mathematical facts, that reason accesses?

If moral facts are like mathematical facts, are they necessary truths, true in all possible worlds?

Could there be a possible world with different moral truths, or are moral truths the same in every possible world?

If moral truths are necessary, how do they connect with the contingent world of human action?

Is the application of necessary moral truths to contingent situations itself a contingent matter, involving judgment rather than deduction?

If moral judgment is not deductive, what kind of reasoning is it — analogical, phronetic, dialectical?

Can the kind of reasoning involved in moral judgment be formalized, or does it essentially resist formalization?

If moral reasoning resists formalization, does this make it less rational, or does it reveal the limits of formal rationality?

---

If reason constructs morality, is the construction arbitrary, or is it constrained?

What constrains moral construction — the nature of reason itself, the nature of human beings, or the conditions of social life?

If the nature of reason constrains moral construction, does reason have a nature, or is reason itself a construction?

If reason is a construction, what constructs it — culture, biology, history?

Is the claim that reason is a construction itself a rational claim, and if so, does it undermine itself?

Can reason be both constructed and authoritative, or does the acknowledgment of construction destroy authority?

Is authority always a claim to be unconstructed, natural, given — and is this claim always false?

If all authority is constructed, does morality lose its authority?

Can morality have authority without claiming to be unconstructed?

Is there a form of authority that honestly acknowledges its own construction and remains authoritative nonetheless?

---

Can a purely rational being be moral, or does morality require more than reason?

If morality requires more than reason, what else does it require — emotion, embodiment, sociality, vulnerability?

Does Kant's purely rational morality leave out something essential, or does it correctly identify the core of morality as rational?

If Kant is right that morality is rational, why do purely rational moral agents seem cold, inhuman, even frightening?

Is the coldness of purely rational morality a defect of the theory or a defect of our expectations?

Do we want morality to be warm, and if so, is this a legitimate moral demand or merely a psychological preference?

Can a moral theory that satisfies reason but offends feeling be adequate?

If feeling has a legitimate claim against purely rational morality, does this mean feeling has cognitive content — that it reveals something reason misses?

What does feeling reveal that reason misses — particularity, context, the irreducible singularity of the moral situation?

Can reason accommodate particularity, or is the universal by its nature blind to the particular?

---

Is the moral law a law of reason, a law of nature, or a law of neither?

If the moral law is a law of reason, does every rational being recognize it, and if not, does this count against its rationality?

Can a rational being fail to recognize a rational law, and if so, is the failure one of reason or one of will?

If the failure is one of will, does the will have its own reasons — reasons that resist the moral law?

Are the reasons of the will always bad reasons, or can the will have legitimate reasons for resisting what reason commands?

If the will can legitimately resist reason, does this mean reason is not sovereign in the moral domain?

What would it mean for reason not to be sovereign in the moral domain — would it mean that morality is partly irrational?

Can morality be partly irrational and still be authoritative?

Is there a rationality broader than calculative or deductive reason — a rationality that includes what formal reason excludes?

If such a broader rationality exists, is it still properly called "reason," or does it need a different name?

## Morality and Emotion

Is morality grounded in emotion, or is emotion merely morality's accompaniment?

---

Do moral emotions — guilt, shame, indignation, compassion — reveal moral truths, or do they merely reflect cultural conditioning?

If moral emotions reveal moral truths, what is the mechanism of revelation — how does a feeling disclose a fact?

Can a feeling be true or false, accurate or inaccurate, in the way that a belief can?

If feelings can be accurate or inaccurate, what are they accurate or inaccurate about — states of affairs, values, the significance of situations?

Is the significance of a situation an objective feature of the situation, or is it projected by the emotional response?

If significance is projected, does this mean that without emotion the world would be morally flat — without salience, without mattering?

Does mattering require a subject to whom things matter, or can things matter independently of any subject?

If mattering requires a subject, does morality exist only where subjects exist?

Does the subject-dependence of mattering make morality subjective, or is it possible for subject-dependent properties to be objective?

Can something be both subject-dependent and objective — can there be objective facts about what matters to subjects?

---

Is empathy the foundation of morality, or is morality possible without empathy?

If empathy is the foundation, what happens to morality in cases where empathy fails — toward strangers, toward enemies, toward those too different to understand?

Can morality extend beyond the reach of empathy, and if so, what extends it — reason, principle, duty?

If reason must supplement empathy, does this mean that empathy is insufficient as a moral foundation?

Can something be foundational without being sufficient?

Is the foundation of a building sufficient for the building — or does the foundation merely make the building possible without determining its shape?

Does morality have a shape, and if so, who or what determines it?

If morality's shape is determined by culture, history, and circumstance, is the shape contingent?

Can something contingent in shape have a necessary foundation?

If morality has a necessary emotional foundation but a contingent cultural shape, is morality necessary or contingent?

---

Is anger ever morally appropriate, or is the moral ideal one of equanimity?

If anger is sometimes morally appropriate, what makes it appropriate — the justice of its cause, the proportionality of its expression, or something else?

Can disproportionate anger at genuine injustice be morally good, or does disproportion always constitute a moral defect?

Is the demand for proportionality in emotion a moral demand or an aesthetic one?

Can the moral and the aesthetic overlap in the domain of emotion — can an emotion be both morally right and aesthetically ugly?

If morally right emotions can be aesthetically ugly, does morality sometimes demand ugliness?

Does the demand for moral beauty — for grace, for dignity, for composure — sometimes conflict with the demand for moral truth?

If moral beauty and moral truth conflict, which takes precedence?

Is this a genuine conflict, or does true morality always have a kind of beauty, even if it is not the beauty of composure?

Is the beauty of moral outrage a genuine beauty, or is "beauty" being stretched beyond its proper meaning?

---

Can love be a moral emotion, or is love amoral — neither moral nor immoral but outside the moral domain?

If love is a moral emotion, does it generate obligations, and are these obligations the same as obligations generated by duty?

Are obligations generated by love stronger or weaker than obligations generated by duty?

If they are stronger, does love trump duty, and if so, can love justify what duty prohibits?

Can a parent's love for their child justify preferential treatment that violates impartial morality?

Is partial love moral, or does morality require impartiality?

If morality requires impartiality, does it require the impossible — that we love all beings equally?

Is the demand for equal love coherent, or does love by its nature discriminate?

If love discriminates, and morality demands impartiality, is love always at least partly immoral?

Can something be immoral and indispensable at the same time?

## Morality and Intuition

Are moral intuitions a source of knowledge, or are they just strong feelings mistaken for insights?

---

If moral intuitions are a source of knowledge, what do they provide knowledge of — moral facts, moral principles, the morally relevant features of situations?

Can intuition provide knowledge of facts, or does knowledge of facts require inference, evidence, or proof?

If intuition provides non-inferential knowledge, is this knowledge foundational — the bedrock on which moral reasoning rests?

If moral intuitions are foundational, can they be wrong, and if they can be wrong, in what sense are they foundational?

Can a foundation be fallible — can the bedrock shift?

If moral intuitions are fallible, how do we correct them — by other intuitions, by reason, by experience?

If we correct intuitions by other intuitions, does this process have a stopping point, or does it regress?

If we correct intuitions by reason, does reason have access to moral truths that intuition lacks?

If reason has independent access to moral truths, why do we need intuition at all?

Is intuition not a source of knowledge but a heuristic — a shortcut that is usually but not always reliable?

---

Do all moral theories ultimately rest on intuitions, or can moral theories be justified without appealing to intuition at some point?

If consequentialism rests on the intuition that consequences matter, and deontology rests on the intuition that certain acts are intrinsically right or wrong, does every moral theory have an intuitive foundation?

If so, does the contest between moral theories reduce to a contest between foundational intuitions?

Can foundational intuitions be evaluated, or are they the point at which evaluation stops?

If foundational intuitions cannot be evaluated, does moral philosophy reach bedrock or reach an impasse?

Is there a difference between bedrock and an impasse — between having reached the bottom and being stuck?

Can moral philosophy make progress if it rests on irreconcilable intuitions?

What would progress look like in this case — deeper understanding of the intuitions, even without resolution?

Is understanding without resolution a legitimate philosophical achievement, or is philosophy obligated to resolve?

Does the obligation to resolve itself rest on an intuition — the intuition that philosophical questions must have answers?

---

Can moral intuitions be culturally specific and yet track universal truths?

If intuitions are shaped by culture, does this undermine their claim to truth, or does culture serve as a lens that can focus as well as distort?

Can we distinguish culturally specific distortions from culturally specific insights?

What criterion would distinguish an insight from a distortion — consistency with reason, convergence across cultures, evolutionary fitness?

If cultures converge on certain moral intuitions, does convergence indicate truth?

Could convergence be explained by common evolutionary pressures rather than common moral truths?

If evolutionary pressures explain convergence, does this explain away the intuitions or explain them?

Is there a stable distinction between explaining and explaining away?

Does any evolutionary explanation of morality automatically debunk morality, or can evolution be a means by which moral truth is tracked?

If evolution tracks moral truth, does this require moral truth to exist independently of evolution — does tracking require something to be tracked?

---

What happens when intuitions conflict — when one intuition says an act is permissible and another says it is forbidden?

Is the trolley problem a genuine test of moral intuitions, or is it an artificial scenario that distorts the intuitions it claims to test?

Do thought experiments in ethics reveal deep moral truths or generate puzzles that have no bearing on real moral life?

If thought experiments have no bearing on real moral life, does this invalidate much of contemporary moral philosophy?

Can a field that relies on thought experiments with no practical bearing be a genuine inquiry into how to live?

Is the question "how should one live?" the fundamental question of ethics, or is it one question among many?

If it is the fundamental question, should ethics be primarily practical, and does the emphasis on theory and thought experiments represent a wrong turn?

Can practical ethics proceed without theory, or does every practical judgment presuppose a theoretical framework?

Is the theory implicit in every practical judgment, and if so, does making it explicit change the judgment?

Does explicit theory improve moral practice, or does it introduce distortions that unreflective practice avoids?

## Morality and the Body

Is the body a moral subject, a moral object, or both?

---

Does moral experience begin with the body — with the vulnerability of flesh, the susceptibility to pain, the capacity for pleasure?

If moral experience begins with the body, is morality fundamentally about bodies — about their protection, their flourishing, their interaction?

Can morality be about anything other than bodies, or do all moral concerns ultimately reduce to concerns about embodied beings?

Do rights attach to bodies, to minds, to persons, or to something else entirely?

If rights attach to persons, and persons are embodied, does the body set the boundary of the person?

Where does the body end — at the skin, at the reach of the senses, at the extent of the body's effects on the world?

If the body's boundary is unclear, is the boundary of the moral subject also unclear?

Does the unclarity of bodily boundaries create moral ambiguity — for instance, in questions about when life begins and ends?

Are beginning-of-life and end-of-life moral questions ultimately questions about bodies, or about something beyond the body?

If they are about something beyond the body — the soul, the mind, the person — how is this something beyond the body related to the body?

---

Does the body have its own morality — a morality of health, of care, of physical integrity?

Is bodily integrity a moral value, and if so, is it absolute or can it be overridden?

If bodily integrity can be overridden, what overrides it — consent, necessity, the greater good?

Can someone consent to the violation of their own bodily integrity, and if so, does consent make the violation permissible?

Is there something that bodily integrity protects that even consent cannot waive — a value so fundamental that it cannot be voluntarily surrendered?

If there is such a value, what is it — dignity, humanity, the sanctity of life?

Is the distinction between judging practices and judging cultures sustainable?

If practices can be judged without judging cultures, does this provide a framework for cross-cultural moral critique?

Is cross-cultural moral critique always risky — always in danger of imposing one culture's values on another?

Does the risk of imposition make cross-cultural critique wrong, or does the risk need to be managed rather than avoided?

Can moral courage include the courage to critique across cultures, even at the risk of being wrong?

Is the willingness to be wrong a moral virtue, and if so, how does it relate to conviction?

Can one be both willing to be wrong and deeply convicted — and is this tension productive or paralyzing?

---

Does moral progress have a direction — is morality moving toward something?

If morality is moving toward something, what is the destination — universal justice, universal compassion, universal flourishing?

Can morality have a destination without having a designer — can moral progress be teleological without being theological?

Is secular teleology coherent — can there be purpose without a purposer?

If secular teleology is coherent, does moral progress have a secular telos?

What would a secular moral telos look like — and would achieving it end moral life?

If achieving the telos ends moral life, is the telos desirable?

Can the end of moral life be the goal of moral life — can morality aim at its own obsolescence?

Is the idea of morality making itself obsolete paradoxical, inspiring, or terrifying?

Does the response to the idea — whether it inspires or terrifies — reveal something about one's deepest moral commitments?

## Morality and Tradition

Is moral tradition a source of wisdom or an obstacle to progress?

---

Does tradition preserve moral truths that would be lost without it?

If tradition preserves moral truths, are these truths accessible through reason alone, or only through tradition?

If some moral truths are accessible only through tradition, does this give tradition an authority that reason cannot override?

If tradition has authority that reason cannot override, is this a dangerous claim — licensing all sorts of practices simply because they are traditional?

Can tradition's authority be limited — can one respect tradition while also criticizing it?

If tradition can be criticized, by what standard — by reason, by competing traditions, by moral intuition?

If tradition is criticized by reason, and reason cannot access the truths that tradition preserves, is the criticism necessarily incomplete?

Can incomplete criticism be valid — can one criticize what one does not fully understand?

Is the claim that outsiders cannot understand a tradition a legitimate defense or a strategy for avoiding criticism?

Does every claim of incomprehensibility serve as a shield against critique, and if so, is the shield sometimes justified?

---

Is the break with tradition ever morally required?

If the break with tradition is sometimes required, what justifies it — the discovery of new moral truths, the recognition of old moral errors, or the demand of new circumstances?

Can moral truths be new, or is every moral truth already present in some tradition?

If every moral truth is already present in some tradition, is moral innovation impossible — is all moral progress really the recovery of forgotten truths?

Is the narrative of moral recovery more accurate than the narrative of moral invention?

If moral truths are recovered rather than invented, does this mean that the moral future is actually the moral past?

Is the idea that the moral future is the moral past conservative, nostalgic, or simply wrong?

Can the past contain moral truths that the present has lost?

If the present has lost moral truths, what caused the loss — modernization, secularization, individualization?

Can lost moral truths be recovered, and if so, what is the process of recovery?

---

Does tradition create moral obligations — does one owe something to the traditions that have formed one?

If one owes something to one's traditions, does this obligation constrain moral freedom?

Can moral freedom coexist with the obligation to tradition, or does freedom require the ability to break with tradition?

If freedom requires the ability to break, does the obligation to tradition undermine freedom?

Can an obligation that undermines freedom be a genuine moral obligation?

Is freedom a necessary condition for moral obligation, or can there be obligations that restrict freedom — including the freedom to reject the obligation?

Are there obligations one cannot reject without ceasing to be who one is — obligations constitutive of identity?

If identity-constitutive obligations cannot be rejected, are they truly obligations, or are they more like facts about the self?

Can a fact about the self be an obligation — can who one is determine what one must do?

If who one is determines what one must do, is morality essentially self-expression — the expression of an identity one did not choose?

## Morality and Relativism

Is moral relativism true, and if so, what follows?

---

Does the existence of moral disagreement prove moral relativism, or only that moral knowledge is difficult?

If moral disagreement proves relativism, does scientific disagreement prove scientific relativism?

If scientific disagreement does not prove scientific relativism, why would moral disagreement prove moral relativism?

Is the analogy between science and morality valid — are moral truths sufficiently similar to scientific truths to draw parallels?

If moral truths are different in kind from scientific truths, how are they different — in their subject matter, their mode of verification, their objectivity?

If moral truths cannot be verified the way scientific truths can, does this make them less real?

Does reality require verifiability, or can real things be unverifiable?

If real things can be unverifiable, is the demand for verification in ethics misplaced?

Is the demand for verification in ethics a product of scientism — the illegitimate extension of scientific standards to non-scientific domains?

Can ethics generate its own standards of truth, independent of scientific standards?

---

If moral relativism is true, can moral criticism across cultures be legitimate?

If cross-cultural moral criticism is illegitimate, does this mean that no culture can be criticized by outsiders?

If no culture can be criticized by outsiders, does this immunize every culture from moral critique?

Is the immunization of culture from critique a morally dangerous consequence of relativism?

If dangerous consequences do not refute a theory, is the danger of relativism irrelevant to its truth?

Can the consequences of a moral theory count against it — can we reject a theory because its consequences are unacceptable?

If we can reject a theory for its consequences, is this a consequentialist form of reasoning applied to moral theories themselves?

Does applying consequentialism to moral theories create a regress — what justifies the consequentialist meta-theory?

Is a regress of justification unavoidable in moral philosophy, or can it be stopped?

If the regress cannot be stopped, does moral philosophy rest on nothing, and if so, can it rest on nothing and still stand?

---

Is the appeal of relativism moral — does it seem more tolerant, more humble, more respectful than moral absolutism?

If relativism seems humble, is this appearance genuine, or does relativism paradoxically impose its own moral standard — the standard of tolerance?

If relativism imposes the standard of tolerance, is it self-refuting — does it violate its own principle by asserting that tolerance is universally required?

Is the self-refutation of relativism a decisive objection, or is it merely a logical puzzle that relativists can work around?

Can relativists consistently advocate tolerance, and if so, how?

Is the consistent relativist indifferent rather than tolerant — does consistency require not caring rather than actively respecting?

Is moral indifference worse than moral intolerance — is the person who does not care worse than the person who cares incorrectly?

Can caring incorrectly be better than not caring at all?

Is the choice between relativism and absolutism a false dilemma — is there a third option?

What would a third option look like — moral pluralism, moral particularism, moral contextualism?

## Morality and Universalism

Is morality universal — does it apply to all beings at all times?

---

If morality is universal, why does it appear so different across cultures and historical periods?

Does the appearance of difference mask an underlying unity — do all moral systems share certain core principles?

If all moral systems share core principles, what are they — prohibitions against murder, theft, lying?

If the core principles are thin — prohibiting only the most basic harms — is the universal morality too thin to be useful?

Can a thin morality be supplemented by thick local moralities without undermining its universality?

Does the supplementation of universal morality by local morality create a two-tier system — and is a two-tier moral system coherent?

If a two-tier system is coherent, does the universal level override the local level in cases of conflict?

If the universal overrides the local, is local morality merely provisional — valid only until it conflicts with universal principles?

If local morality is provisional, does this deprive it of its authority — can something be authoritative and provisional?

Is the tension between the authority of local morality and the authority of universal morality irresolvable?

---

Is the universality of morality a Western conceit — an attempt to impose Western values on the world under the guise of universality?

If universalism is a Western conceit, does this invalidate it — can a culturally specific idea be universally true?

Is the concept of universal truth itself culturally specific?

If the concept of universal truth is culturally specific, is the claim that it is culturally specific self-undermining — does the claim apply to itself?

Does the self-referential paradox of cultural relativism about truth show that some truths must be universal?

If some truths must be universal, are moral truths among them?

What would a genuinely universal morality look like — minimal, formal, procedural, or substantive?

Can a substantive universal morality avoid being culturally biased?

If a substantive universal morality cannot avoid bias, should we settle for a formal universal morality?

Is a formal universal morality — one that specifies only the form of moral reasoning, not its content — sufficient for moral life?

---

Can the universality of morality be established without appealing to God, nature, or reason?

If morality's universality cannot be established, can morality still function as if it were universal?

Is the "as if" of moral universality sufficient — can we act as though morality is universal without believing it?

Does acting as though morality is universal while not believing it make us moral hypocrites?

Is there a difference between philosophical uncertainty about universality and practical commitment to universality?

Can one be practically committed to universality while philosophically uncertain about it?

Is the practical commitment to universality what moral life requires — not certainty, but commitment?

If commitment without certainty is what moral life requires, is moral life essentially a matter of faith?

Is moral faith analogous to religious faith, and if so, does this undermine secular morality's claim to stand on reason alone?

Can reason stand alone, or does it always rest on something beyond itself — commitment, faith, trust?

## Morality and Nihilism

What is moral nihilism, and is it a coherent position?

---

If moral nihilism claims that there are no moral truths, is this claim itself a moral truth?

If the claim is not a moral truth, what kind of truth is it — a metaphysical truth, an epistemic truth, a therapeutic observation?

Can a non-moral truth have moral implications?

If the non-existence of moral truth has moral implications, does moral nihilism undermine itself by having moral significance?

Can moral nihilism coexist with moral behavior — can one believe that nothing is right or wrong and still act morally?

If a nihilist acts morally, are they acting from habit, from convention, from emotional inclination, or from something else?

If a nihilist acts morally from habit, does habit suffice for morality, and if so, is moral belief unnecessary?

If moral belief is unnecessary, does moral philosophy serve any purpose?

Is the purpose of moral philosophy to generate moral beliefs, or to understand moral life — and is understanding possible without belief?

Can understanding morality be morally valuable even if it does not generate moral beliefs?

---

Is nihilism the natural endpoint of moral inquiry — does moral philosophy, pursued to its conclusion, undermine morality?

If moral inquiry undermines morality, should moral inquiry be curtailed for moral reasons?

Can morality demand the curtailment of inquiry, and if so, is this a form of intellectual cowardice?

Is intellectual cowardice a moral failing, and if so, does morality sometimes demand both honesty and self-limitation?

Can the demands for honesty and self-limitation coexist, or does honesty require following inquiry wherever it leads?

If honesty requires following inquiry wherever it leads, and inquiry leads to nihilism, must the honest person be a nihilist?

If the honest person must be a nihilist, is nihilism a moral achievement — the result of unflinching honesty?

Can nihilism be a moral achievement if it denies the existence of moral achievements?

Is the paradox of moral nihilism — that it can be morally arrived at but denies the moral — insoluble?

Does the insolubility of the paradox reveal something about the relationship between morality and truth — that they cannot be fully reconciled?

---

Does nihilism follow from the recognition that moral beliefs are useful fictions?

If moral beliefs are useful fictions, is belief in them justified by their utility?

If belief is justified by utility, is this a pragmatist position, a nihilist position, or something else?

Can pragmatism and nihilism coexist — can one believe that morality is a useful fiction and that there are no moral truths?

If pragmatism and nihilism coexist, is the resulting position livable — can one live as though morality matters while believing it does not?

Is the ability to live a position a test of its truth, or only of its livability?

Can a position be livable and false?

If livability does not indicate truth, what does — coherence, evidence, explanatory power, intuitive force?

Does moral truth require the same kind of evidence as empirical truth, or is moral evidence of a different kind?

If moral evidence is of a different kind, what kind — testimonial, experiential, phenomenological, or none at all?

## Morality and Meaning

Does morality give life meaning, or does meaning give morality its force?

---

If morality gives life meaning, can a life without morality be meaningful?

If a life without morality can be meaningful — meaningful through art, love, achievement — does morality lose its claim to be necessary for meaning?

If morality is not necessary for meaning, is it merely one source of meaning among many?

If morality is one source of meaning among many, does this lower its status — from supreme value to one value among others?

Can morality be demoted from supreme value without being degraded?

If morality can be one value among many, does it still have the authority to override other values?

Can a non-supreme value override other values, or does overriding require supremacy?

If overriding requires supremacy, and morality is not supreme, then morality cannot override — but this seems wrong, since morality often does override.

Does the phenomenological experience of morality overriding other values prove morality's supremacy, or is the experience misleading?

Can the experience of moral override be an illusion — can we feel that morality overrides when it actually does not?

---

If meaning gives morality its force, what gives meaning its force?

Is meaning self-grounding — does meaning need no further ground — or does it rest on something else?

If meaning rests on something else, what — purpose, narrative, God, human nature?

If meaning rests on God, does the death of God entail the death of meaning?

If the death of God entails the death of meaning, and the death of meaning entails the death of morality, does atheism entail nihilism?

If atheism does not entail nihilism, how is meaning possible without God?

Can meaning be created rather than discovered, and if so, is created meaning as real as discovered meaning?

Is the distinction between created and discovered meaning stable, or does all meaning involve both creation and discovery?

If meaning involves both creation and discovery, does the created element make it subjective and the discovered element make it objective?

Can something be simultaneously subjective and objective — and is morality such a thing?

---

Does the search for meaning presuppose that meaning exists to be found?

If meaning does not exist to be found, is the search futile?

Can a futile search be valuable — can the search for meaning that does not exist itself be meaningful?

If the search is meaningful even when unsuccessful, does this mean that meaning is generated by the act of searching?

Is meaning-generation through searching a genuine phenomenon, or is it a consolation offered to those who have found nothing?

Can consolation be true — can a belief that consoles also be accurate?

Is the relationship between consolation and truth one of tension or one of potential harmony?

If consolation and truth can harmonize, does the moral life represent this harmony — a life that is both consoling and true?

If the moral life is both consoling and true, is it the best life — the life most worth living?

Is the question of the life most worth living ultimately a moral question, a philosophical question, or a question that exceeds both?

## The Recursive Structure of Morality

Does morality contain within itself the tools for its own critique, or must critique come from outside?

---

If morality contains within itself the tools for its own critique, is morality self-correcting?

If morality is self-correcting, does it need moral philosophy — or can morality correct itself without philosophical assistance?

If morality can correct itself without philosophy, what role does moral philosophy play — clarification, systematization, or something else?

Is clarification a moral task, or is it a purely intellectual task with moral implications?

Can an intellectual task have moral implications without being a moral task?

Is the distinction between intellectual and moral tasks clear, or do all intellectual tasks concerning morality become moral tasks?

If all intellectual tasks concerning morality are moral tasks, is moral philosophy a form of moral practice?

If moral philosophy is a form of moral practice, are moral philosophers morally responsible for the quality of their philosophizing?

Is bad moral philosophy morally wrong, or merely intellectually deficient?

Can intellectual deficiency in moral matters constitute a moral failing?

---

Is morality recursive — does it apply to itself?

If morality applies to itself, is there a morality of morality — a meta-morality that evaluates moral systems?

If there is a meta-morality, does it presuppose a meta-meta-morality, generating an infinite regress?

If the regress is infinite, does this undermine the project of moral justification?

If moral justification is impossible, can morality proceed without justification?

Can unjustified morality be authoritative — can we follow moral demands whose justification we do not possess?

Is the following of unjustified demands a form of faith, a form of habit, or a form of practical wisdom?

If it is a form of practical wisdom, does practical wisdom precede theoretical justification?

If practical wisdom precedes theoretical justification, does this mean that we live morally before we understand morality?

Does the priority of living over understanding mean that morality is ultimately a practice, not a theory — and does this resolve or merely relocate the problem of justification?

---

Does every moral claim contain an implicit reference to the conditions of its own validity?

If moral claims refer to their own conditions of validity, can these conditions be made explicit without distortion?

If making conditions explicit distorts them, is morality essentially implicit — known only in practice, never in theory?

If morality is essentially implicit, does moral philosophy always misrepresent morality?

If moral philosophy always misrepresents morality, is it a necessary misrepresentation — necessary for reflection, for criticism, for improvement?

Can a necessary misrepresentation be corrected, or is it permanent by nature?

If the misrepresentation is permanent, does moral philosophy live in permanent tension with moral life?

Is the tension between philosophy and life productive — does it generate insight — or destructive — does it generate confusion?

Can the same tension be both productive and destructive?

Does the ambivalence of the tension between moral philosophy and moral life mirror the ambivalence at the heart of morality itself — the ambivalence between demand and incapacity, between ideal and real, between what ought to be and what is?

---

Does the question "What is morality?" change morality by posing it?

If the question changes morality, does morality before the question differ from morality after?

If morality changes when questioned, is the pre-reflective morality more authentic than the post-reflective morality?

If pre-reflective morality is more authentic, is the loss of innocence through reflection a moral fall?

If reflection is a fall, should we avoid reflection — and is this recommendation self-defeating, since it requires reflection to formulate?

Is the self-defeating character of anti-reflective advice an argument for reflection?

If reflection is unavoidable, must we accept that morality is always already contaminated by reflection?

If morality is always already reflective, does this mean that the natural, pre-reflective morality we imagine was always a myth?

If pre-reflective morality is a myth, is the myth morally useful?

Does the usefulness of the myth of pre-reflective morality reveal something final about morality itself — that morality is the practice of navigating between what we can never fully understand and what we can never stop trying to understand?
