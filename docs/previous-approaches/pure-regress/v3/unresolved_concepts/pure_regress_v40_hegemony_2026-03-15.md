# Recursive Causal Interrogation: Hegemony

## Hegemony

Is there a form of power so pervasive that those subject to it cannot recognize it as power at all?

---

What does hegemony mean, and does the word name a single phenomenon or a cluster of related but distinct mechanisms of dominance?

Is hegemony a kind of rule, a kind of influence, a kind of consent, or something that dissolves the boundaries between all three?

If hegemony is distinguished from mere domination, what is it about hegemony that domination lacks?

Does domination operate through force while hegemony operates through meaning, or is this distinction too clean?

Can force itself become meaningful, and can meaning itself become forceful?

If coercion and consent are the two poles of political power, does hegemony sit between them, or does it reveal that the distinction between coercion and consent was never stable?

Is consent that has been manufactured still consent?

If manufactured consent is not genuine consent, what would genuine consent look like, and has it ever existed?

Does the demand for genuine consent presuppose a subject free from all influence, and does such a subject exist anywhere outside philosophical abstraction?

If no one is free from influence, does the concept of consent lose its meaning, or does it merely require redefinition?

---

Who coined the concept of hegemony in its modern political sense, and does the origin of a concept constrain its valid application?

Did Gramsci invent hegemony or discover it?

If he discovered it, was hegemony operating before anyone named it, and does naming a form of power alter its operation?

Does the concept of hegemony itself function hegemonically by organizing how people think about power?

If Gramsci developed the concept in prison, does the fact that he was imprisoned — subjected to coercion — while theorizing consent-based power reveal something about the relationship between the two?

Can a prisoner see hegemony more clearly than a free citizen, because the prisoner has been expelled from the domain where consent operates?

Does exclusion from hegemonic order grant critical insight, or does it merely produce a different kind of blindness?

Is the outsider's perspective on hegemony more accurate or simply differently distorted?

---

What did Gramsci mean by the distinction between political society and civil society, and does this distinction hold under contemporary conditions?

Is political society the domain of coercion and civil society the domain of consent, or do both domains contain elements of each?

If the state uses force and civil society manufactures consent, where does hegemony reside — in the state, in civil society, or in the relationship between them?

Can hegemony exist without a state?

Did hegemonic relations exist in pre-state societies, or does hegemony require the particular configuration of power that states produce?

If hegemony requires institutions — schools, churches, media, family structures — can it survive the weakening of those institutions?

Does the internet strengthen or weaken hegemonic power, or does it transform hegemony into something the classical concept cannot capture?

Is algorithmic curation a form of hegemony, or does it operate through a mechanism so different from Gramscian hegemony that a new concept is needed?

If a new concept is needed, does that mean hegemony has become obsolete, or merely that it needs to be updated?

Can a concept be updated without becoming a different concept?

---

What is the relationship between hegemony and ideology?

Is hegemony the mechanism by which ideology becomes dominant, or is ideology the content that hegemony delivers?

Can there be hegemony without ideology — a structuring of consent that operates below the level of ideas?

If hegemony can operate through affect, habit, and bodily practice rather than through beliefs and ideas, does calling it "ideological" mislead?

Does the body consent differently than the mind, and if so, which form of consent does hegemony primarily secure?

Can one's body be hegemonized while one's mind resists, or does the division between bodily compliance and mental resistance eventually collapse?

Is the factory worker whose body performs the required motions while whose mind harbors revolutionary thoughts genuinely resisting hegemony, or is the compliance of the body the more fundamental form of consent?

Does hegemony ultimately target the body or the soul?

Did Foucault's concept of biopower replace Gramsci's hegemony, or does it describe a different layer of the same phenomenon?

---

What does it mean to say that hegemony operates through common sense?

Is common sense the sediment of past hegemonic struggles, or is it a transhistorical feature of human social life?

If common sense is historical, whose history does it carry?

Does common sense feel natural because it is natural, or because hegemony has made it feel that way?

Can one distinguish between common sense that reflects genuine shared experience and common sense that reflects the interests of a dominant group disguised as universal truth?

Is the feeling of obviousness itself a hegemonic product?

When something "goes without saying," is that because it is truly self-evident or because hegemony has made questioning it seem absurd?

Does the person who questions what goes without saying appear foolish because they are foolish, or because hegemony protects itself by making its challengers appear foolish?

Is the social cost of questioning common sense — being dismissed as naive, radical, or insane — itself a hegemonic mechanism?

Does hegemony maintain itself not primarily through persuasion but through the stigmatization of dissent?

---

If common sense is hegemonic, is philosophy — which questions common sense — inherently counter-hegemonic?

Or can philosophy itself become hegemonic when it establishes its own unquestioned assumptions?

Is the philosophical tradition dominated by certain voices, and does this domination constitute a hegemony within the domain that claims to question hegemony?

Can the canon be hegemonic?

Does the fact that certain thinkers are read and others are forgotten reflect intellectual merit, or does it reflect hegemonic selection pressures?

If Gramsci is now canonical, has his critique of hegemony been absorbed into the very hegemonic order it was meant to challenge?

Does the institutionalization of critical theory neutralize it?

Can a radical concept survive its incorporation into the university curriculum, or does the curriculum domesticate everything it touches?

Is the domestication of radical ideas a feature of hegemony or a failure of the ideas themselves?

---

What is intellectual and moral leadership, and how does it differ from intellectual and moral domination?

Did Gramsci believe that the proletariat could achieve hegemony, or only that the bourgeoisie had achieved it?

If the proletariat can achieve hegemony, does that mean hegemony is a neutral mechanism that any class can wield, or does proletarian hegemony differ in kind from bourgeois hegemony?

Can a hegemony of the oppressed avoid reproducing the structures of domination that characterized the hegemony of the oppressor?

Does the aspiration to hegemony corrupt any movement that pursues it, or is hegemony an unavoidable feature of all political organization?

If hegemony is unavoidable, does the question become not whether to exercise hegemony but how to exercise it justly?

Can hegemony ever be just, or does justice require the abolition of hegemonic relations altogether?

What would a post-hegemonic society look like, and is the concept of a post-hegemonic society itself a hegemonic fantasy — an ideal that serves the interests of those who produce it?

Is the dream of a world without domination a form of domination over those who find meaning in hierarchy?

---

What is the role of intellectuals in hegemonic reproduction?

Does every social group produce its own intellectuals, or are intellectuals a distinct social stratum with their own interests?

What did Gramsci mean by organic intellectuals, and how do they differ from traditional intellectuals?

Is the organic intellectual embedded in a class and articulating its worldview, while the traditional intellectual claims to stand above class altogether?

Is the claim to stand above class itself a class position — the position of those whose material comfort allows them to ignore material conditions?

Does the traditional intellectual's claim to universality mask a particular interest?

If all intellectual production is interested, does that mean objectivity is impossible, or merely that objectivity must be understood differently?

Can objectivity be reconceived as the effort to account for one's own situatedness rather than the pretense of having no situation?

Is this reconception itself a hegemonic move by situated thinkers seeking to delegitimize their unsituated competitors?

Does the debate about objectivity reproduce the hegemonic struggle it describes?

---

What is the relationship between hegemony and language?

Does the dominant group control not just what is said but what can be said?

If certain thoughts are literally unthinkable within a given language, does language itself function as a hegemonic structure?

Is the spread of English a form of linguistic hegemony, and if so, what is lost when other languages decline?

Does a language carry a worldview, and does the dominance of one language impose one worldview at the expense of others?

Can one resist linguistic hegemony by speaking in the dominant language, or does resistance require speaking from outside it?

If resistance requires a language of its own, who creates that language, and does its creation constitute a new hegemony?

Is the language of resistance always parasitic on the language of power, or can it achieve genuine autonomy?

Can a pidgin or creole — a language born from the collision of dominator and dominated — become a site of counter-hegemonic expression?

Does the concept of counter-hegemony make sense, or does counter-hegemony inevitably become a new hegemony the moment it succeeds?

## Consent and Coercion

Is the distinction between consent and coercion the foundation of the concept of hegemony, or is hegemony precisely the concept that undermines that distinction?

---

Can consent be given under conditions of inequality, or does inequality automatically vitiate consent?

If inequality vitiates consent, has anyone ever truly consented to any political arrangement, given that perfect equality has never existed?

Does the impossibility of perfect equality make consent a useful fiction rather than a factual description?

Is a useful fiction still useful if everyone knows it is a fiction?

Does hegemony depend on people not knowing that their consent is manufactured, or can hegemony survive the exposure of its own mechanisms?

Can one know that one's preferences have been shaped by hegemonic forces and still authentically hold those preferences?

Is the smoker who knows that advertising created her desire for cigarettes still freely choosing to smoke?

Does knowledge of manipulation undo the manipulation, or does it merely add a layer of awareness to an unchanged behavioral pattern?

If knowing about hegemony does not free one from hegemony, what does?

Is there any exit from hegemonic relations, or is the search for an exit itself a hegemonic product — the fantasy of autonomy that keeps subjects compliant?

---

What is coercion, and where does it begin?

Is coercion limited to physical force, or does economic necessity constitute coercion?

If the threat of starvation compels someone to accept exploitative labor conditions, is that coercion or consent?

Does the fact that the worker "chose" to accept the job mean the acceptance was voluntary, or does the absence of viable alternatives render the choice meaningless?

Is a choice between two bad options still a choice?

Does hegemony operate precisely by structuring the field of available options so that all choices lead to outcomes compatible with the dominant interest?

If hegemony controls the menu rather than the selection, is the diner free?

Can one be free within a constrained set of options, or does freedom require the ability to alter the set itself?

Does the ability to imagine options that do not exist constitute a form of freedom, or is imagination without actualization mere consolation?

Is the consolation of imagination something hegemony actively provides — a safety valve that prevents real resistance by offering imaginary alternatives?

---

Does hegemony require active maintenance, or does it persist through inertia once established?

If hegemony requires constant reproduction, who does the work of reproducing it?

Does everyone participate in the reproduction of hegemony, including those who are disadvantaged by it?

If the oppressed participate in their own oppression, is that participation a sign of false consciousness, rational adaptation, or something that escapes both categories?

Is the concept of false consciousness compatible with respect for the agency of those it describes?

Does calling someone's consciousness "false" reproduce the very hierarchy that hegemony critique claims to oppose?

Can one identify hegemonic distortions in others' thinking without claiming epistemic superiority?

Is the claim to see through hegemony always a claim to occupy a privileged position, and is that claim itself hegemonic?

Does the critic of hegemony stand outside hegemony, or does criticism operate within the hegemonic field it describes?

---

If no one stands outside hegemony, how is critique possible?

Does critique require an external standpoint, or can it emerge from contradictions within the hegemonic order itself?

Is hegemony ever internally consistent, or does it always contain contradictions that generate their own critique?

If hegemony contains its own contradictions, does it also contain its own critique, and is that domesticated critique part of how hegemony sustains itself?

Does a system that can absorb criticism become stronger through criticism, the way an immune system becomes stronger through exposure to pathogens?

Is liberal democracy a hegemonic order that has perfected the absorption of critique?

Does the fact that one can criticize liberal democracy within liberal democracy prove its openness or its hegemonic sophistication?

Is tolerance a hegemonic strategy — a way of neutralizing dissent by appearing to welcome it?

Can tolerance coexist with genuine pluralism, or does tolerance always imply a dominant position from which others are tolerated?

Does the tolerant subject occupy a hegemonic position precisely by granting permission for others to exist?

---

What is the relationship between hegemony and legitimacy?

Does hegemonic power become legitimate simply because people accept it, or does legitimacy require something more than acceptance?

If legitimacy requires moral justification, who provides that justification — the powerful, the powerless, or some independent standard?

Is there a standard of legitimacy independent of all power relations, or is every standard produced by and within power relations?

If every standard is produced by power, does that make legitimacy a circular concept — power is legitimate when it meets standards that power itself has set?

Does the circularity of legitimacy invalidate it, or is circularity an inescapable feature of all normative concepts?

Is the search for non-circular legitimacy a form of political theology — the desire for a transcendent foundation that secular politics cannot provide?

Does the death of God leave a legitimacy vacuum that hegemony fills?

Is hegemony what replaces divine right in a secular age?

---

What is the relationship between hegemony and democracy?

Is democracy the antithesis of hegemony, or its most effective form?

Can a democratic majority exercise hegemony over a minority, and if so, is that hegemony legitimate because it is democratic?

Does majority rule ensure that the majority's common sense becomes the common sense, marginalizing minority worldviews?

Is the concept of minority rights an attempt to limit hegemonic democracy, or is it itself a hegemonic imposition by elites who distrust popular sovereignty?

Does popular sovereignty presuppose a unified "people," and is the construction of a unified people itself a hegemonic operation?

Who decides who belongs to "the people" and who does not?

Is the exclusion of certain groups from "the people" constitutive of political community, or is it a contingent injustice that can be overcome?

If every political community requires a boundary between inside and outside, does every democracy contain an irreducible hegemonic kernel?

Can democracy be radicalized to the point where it no longer requires exclusion, or does the attempt to include everyone paradoxically exclude those who depend on exclusion for their identity?

## Normalization and Common Sense

How does the abnormal become normal, and does normalization erase the history of its own production?

---

Is the normal a statistical concept, a moral concept, or a political concept?

If the normal is statistical — simply what most people do — can it carry normative force, or is the move from "most people do X" to "people should do X" always a fallacy?

Does the frequency of a behavior influence its perceived rightness, and if so, is that influence rational or hegemonic?

Is the feeling that something is normal the primary affective mechanism of hegemony?

Does hegemony succeed not when people agree with the dominant order but when they feel that the dominant order is simply how things are?

Is the feeling of inevitability the deepest form of hegemonic capture?

If people feel that the current arrangement is inevitable, does that feeling preclude the imagination of alternatives?

Does the slogan "there is no alternative" describe a reality or produce one?

Is the production of the feeling of inevitability the primary task of hegemonic culture?

Can culture produce the feeling of possibility as effectively as it produces the feeling of inevitability, or is the production of inevitability easier because inertia favors it?

---

How does hegemony relate to habit?

Is habit the micro-mechanism through which hegemony operates at the level of the individual body?

Does the body that habitually defers to authority internalize hegemony more deeply than the mind that consciously endorses it?

Can habits be changed, and is the changing of habits a form of counter-hegemonic practice?

Does the difficulty of changing habits explain the persistence of hegemonic arrangements even after their ideological justifications have been discredited?

If people continue to act as if the old order is natural even after they intellectually reject it, does that mean hegemony resides in the body rather than in the mind?

Is Bourdieu's habitus a redescription of Gramscian hegemony at the level of the individual?

Does the concept of habitus add anything to the concept of hegemony, or does it merely translate a political concept into a sociological one?

Is the translation from political to sociological vocabulary itself a form of depoliticization, and is depoliticization a hegemonic strategy?

Does the social sciences' tendency to describe power in neutral, analytical terms serve hegemony by removing the moral urgency from the description of domination?

---

What counts as common sense, and who decides?

Is common sense the same across cultures, or is each culture's common sense a product of its particular hegemonic history?

If common sense varies across cultures, can any common sense claim universal validity?

Does the claim that one culture's common sense is universally valid constitute cultural hegemony?

Is the project of universal human rights an expression of universal moral truth or a particular Western common sense claiming universal authority?

Can human rights be defended without cultural hegemony, or does their defense inevitably impose one culture's values on others?

Is the accusation of cultural imperialism itself a hegemonic move — a way for local elites to protect their own hegemony from external challenge?

Does the debate between universalism and cultural relativism reproduce hegemonic struggles at the meta-level?

Can one escape the universalism-relativism binary, or is every position on this question necessarily located within one or the other?

Is the claim to have transcended the binary itself a universalist move disguised as transcendence?

---

How does education function as a hegemonic institution?

Does education transmit knowledge or produce subjects?

If education produces subjects, does it produce the subjects that the hegemonic order requires?

Can education be liberatory, or is the concept of liberatory education a contradiction — liberation through an institution designed for subjection?

Did Freire's pedagogy of the oppressed escape hegemonic reproduction, or did it create a new hegemonic pedagogy?

Is critical pedagogy a form of counter-hegemony, or does it produce students who critique hegemony in the approved manner — which is to say, hegemonically?

Can students resist the hegemony of the classroom while remaining within it?

Does the grade constitute a hegemonic mechanism — a technology for converting compliance into credential?

Is the credentialed subject a product of educational hegemony, and does the credential's value depend on the hegemonic order's willingness to recognize it?

If the credential has no value outside the system that produces it, is the credentialed subject free or captured?

---

How does media function as a hegemonic apparatus?

Does media represent reality or produce it?

If media produces the reality it claims to represent, is objectivity in journalism a hegemonic fiction?

Can objectivity be distinguished from the hegemonic center — the position that presents itself as neutral because it occupies the dominant position?

Is the "view from nowhere" actually the view from the hegemonic center?

Does the accusation of media bias presuppose a non-biased position, and is the claim to that position itself biased?

If all media is biased, does that make all media hegemonic, or only the media that denies its bias?

Is the independent media that acknowledges its perspective less hegemonic than the mainstream media that claims objectivity?

Or does the independent media's marginal position simply reflect its failure to achieve hegemony, which it would exercise as readily as the mainstream if given the chance?

Does the desire for influence necessarily become hegemonic, or can one influence without dominating?

## Cultural Hegemony and Everyday Life

Does hegemony penetrate everyday life, or is everyday life the site where hegemony encounters its limits?

---

Is the decision about what to eat a hegemonic matter?

Does the global dominance of certain cuisines reflect culinary excellence or economic power?

Is the fast-food industry a hegemonic force that restructures not only diets but also temporalities, social rituals, and the meaning of nourishment?

Can one resist food hegemony by eating differently, or does the framing of food choice as resistance already capitulate to the hegemonic logic of consumer choice?

Is the slow food movement counter-hegemonic or merely an elite alternative that reinforces class hegemony through culinary distinction?

Does the distinction between good taste and bad taste in food map onto class distinctions, and if so, is taste a hegemonic category?

Can one enjoy fast food without false consciousness, or does the enjoyment itself require critique?

Does the critique of enjoyment participate in hegemony by reproducing the distinction between those who enjoy naively and those who critique knowingly?

Is the knowing subject hegemonically superior to the naive subject, and does this superiority contradict the egalitarian aspirations of hegemony critique?

Does hegemony critique always risk becoming a new form of hegemony exercised by critics over the uncritical?

---

How does hegemony shape the experience of time?

Is the division of time into work time and leisure time a hegemonic structure?

Does the weekend exist because workers need rest, or because hegemony requires periods of consumption to complement periods of production?

Is leisure a form of freedom or a form of capture — time allocated by the hegemonic order for the reproduction of labor power?

Can one experience genuine leisure within a hegemonic order, or is all leisure already colonized by the demands of productivity?

Is the imperative to use leisure productively — to optimize, to self-improve, to hustle — a sign that hegemony has penetrated even the time that was supposed to be free?

Does the concept of "free time" presuppose that most time is unfree, and is this presupposition a hegemonic concession?

If all time were free, would the concept of free time lose its meaning?

Does hegemony depend on the scarcity of freedom, distributing small doses to prevent the demand for more?

Is the weekend a hegemonic compromise — enough freedom to prevent revolution, not enough to threaten the structure?

---

How does hegemony operate through aesthetics?

Does the dominant aesthetic — what counts as beautiful, tasteful, elegant — reflect natural standards or hegemonic impositions?

If beauty standards vary across cultures and historical periods, does that prove they are hegemonic constructions?

Can beauty be both constructed and real, or does construction imply unreality?

Does the experience of beauty carry hegemonic content even when it feels purely personal and spontaneous?

Is the preference for certain body types, skin colors, or facial features a hegemonic preference, and if so, can it be changed?

Does the effort to change beauty standards constitute counter-hegemonic action, or does it merely replace one hegemonic standard with another?

Is the diversification of beauty standards a genuine challenge to hegemony, or is it the hegemonic order's strategy for expanding its market?

Can capitalism absorb every counter-hegemonic aesthetic by commodifying it?

Does the commodification of resistance prove the totality of hegemony, or does it prove that hegemony must constantly respond to challenges it cannot fully neutralize?

---

Is architecture hegemonic?

Do buildings enforce social hierarchies through their design — elevating some, confining others, directing flows of movement and attention?

Is the open-plan office a democratic space or a panopticon?

Does the skyscraper express hegemonic ambition, and if so, whose ambition does it express?

Is public space genuinely public, or is it a hegemonic space designed to accommodate certain uses and exclude others?

Does the bench with armrests that prevent lying down embody hegemonic hostility toward the homeless?

Is hostile architecture a visible form of usually invisible hegemony?

Does the visibility of hostile architecture undermine hegemony by making its mechanisms apparent, or does the blatancy itself communicate that resistance is futile?

Can space be designed counter-hegemonically, and what would counter-hegemonic architecture look like?

Is the commune, the squat, or the occupied factory a counter-hegemonic space, or does its marginality confirm the hegemony it opposes?

---

Does hegemony shape emotional life?

Are the emotions people feel — their fears, desires, satisfactions, resentments — shaped by hegemonic structures?

Is the fear of poverty a natural response to scarcity or a hegemonic affect cultivated to ensure compliance?

Does hegemony generate the anxieties that it then offers to manage?

Is advertising a technology for producing hegemonic desires — desires that serve the dominant order by directing consumption?

Can one desire authentically within a hegemonic order, or is every desire marked by hegemonic conditioning?

If all desire is conditioned, does the distinction between authentic and inauthentic desire collapse?

Does the collapse of the authentic-inauthentic distinction serve hegemony by eliminating the standard against which hegemonic distortion could be measured?

Is the concept of authenticity itself hegemonic — a way of privileging certain desires (coded as natural, original, deep) over others (coded as artificial, derivative, shallow)?

Does the pursuit of authenticity reproduce hegemony by assuming that there is a true self beneath social conditioning, when the self may be social conditioning all the way down?

## Hegemonic Masculinity

Is masculinity a hegemonic formation, and if so, what does it dominate?

---

What does it mean to call a form of masculinity "hegemonic"?

Is hegemonic masculinity the masculinity that most men practice, the masculinity that most men aspire to, or the masculinity that organizes the gender hierarchy?

If most men do not embody hegemonic masculinity, how does it maintain its hegemonic position?

Does hegemonic masculinity rely on the complicity of men who benefit from the patriarchal dividend without personally enacting dominant masculinity?

Is complicity with hegemonic masculinity a form of consent, and is it a Gramscian consent — manufactured, naturalized, and felt as common sense?

Can a man reject hegemonic masculinity while continuing to benefit from the structure it sustains?

Does structural benefit without personal endorsement constitute participation in hegemony?

Is the "good man" who benefits from patriarchy without endorsing it a counter-hegemonic figure or a hegemonic free rider?

Does the distinction between individual goodness and structural complicity dissolve under scrutiny, or does it remain meaningful?

Can individual virtue coexist with structural injustice, or does structural injustice contaminate individual virtue?

---

Is hegemonic masculinity always defined in opposition to femininity, or can it be defined on its own terms?

If hegemonic masculinity requires the subordination of femininity, is it constitutively relational — incapable of existing without something to dominate?

Does the need for a subordinate other reveal that hegemonic masculinity is not a positive identity but a relationship of dominance?

If hegemonic masculinity is a relationship rather than an identity, can it be abolished without abolishing the parties to the relationship?

Does the abolition of hegemonic masculinity require the abolition of masculinity altogether, or only the hegemonic form?

Can non-hegemonic masculinities exist, and would they still be recognizably masculine?

Is the question of what masculinity "really" is a question about nature or about hegemony?

Does biology ground masculinity, or does the appeal to biology serve a hegemonic function by naturalizing a social hierarchy?

If the appeal to biology is hegemonic, does that mean biology is irrelevant, or merely that its relevance has been hegemonically exaggerated?

Can one acknowledge biological difference without that acknowledgment becoming a justification for hierarchy?

---

Does hegemonic masculinity subordinate not only femininity but also non-hegemonic masculinities?

Is the subordination of gay masculinity a constitutive feature of hegemonic masculinity, or a contingent historical association?

If hegemonic masculinity can accommodate gay men — as it increasingly appears to in some contexts — has hegemonic masculinity changed, or has it merely expanded its boundaries while maintaining its structure?

Does inclusion in the hegemonic order neutralize the subversive potential of previously excluded identities?

Is the "masc" gay man who performs hegemonic masculinity reinforcing the structure or proving its flexibility?

Does the flexibility of hegemonic masculinity make it more resilient, absorbing challenges that a rigid hegemony would break against?

Is resilience through adaptation a sign of hegemonic strength or a sign that the hegemony has been fundamentally altered by the adaptation?

Can a hegemony change so much through adaptation that it ceases to be the same hegemony, or does the continuity of structural advantage persist through all surface changes?

Is the persistence of structural advantage the defining feature of hegemony, regardless of changes in its cultural expression?

---

Does hegemonic masculinity shape how men experience their own bodies?

Is the pressure to be physically strong, tall, muscular, or athletic a hegemonic norm, and does it damage men who cannot or will not conform?

Does the damage done to non-conforming men by hegemonic masculinity count as evidence that hegemony harms even those it ostensibly privileges?

Can a system that harms its own beneficiaries be stable, or does the harm it inflicts on privileged subjects generate internal resistance?

Is men's emotional repression a hegemonic demand — a requirement for membership in the dominant gender category?

Does the injunction to suppress vulnerability serve hegemonic masculinity by producing subjects who will not question the order that demands their suffering?

Is the man who cannot cry a hegemonic product, and is his suffering the price of his privilege?

Does the concept of "toxic masculinity" name a corruption of an otherwise healthy masculinity, or does it name what masculinity has always been under hegemonic conditions?

If all existing masculinities have been shaped by hegemonic conditions, is there a non-toxic masculinity waiting to be recovered, or must it be invented?

Can masculinity be reinvented without reinventing the entire gender order, or is the gender order a totality that can only be transformed as a whole?

---

Is feminism a counter-hegemonic project, and if so, has it achieved counter-hegemony or merely reformed the existing hegemony?

Can feminism become hegemonic, and would feminist hegemony differ in kind from patriarchal hegemony?

Does the concept of hegemony apply equally to all forms of dominance, or was it designed to describe class relations and stretched beyond its proper scope when applied to gender?

If hegemony was designed for class analysis, does its application to gender distort either the concept or the phenomenon?

Can a single concept adequately describe the dominance of the bourgeoisie over the proletariat and the dominance of masculinity over femininity, or do these require different analytical tools?

Is the tendency to apply hegemony to everything a sign of the concept's power or a sign of intellectual laziness?

Does the universal application of a concept dilute it?

If hegemony explains everything, does it explain nothing?

Is the accusation that a concept explains nothing when applied to everything itself a hegemonic move — a way of protecting dominant interests by discrediting the analytical tools designed to expose them?

## Counter-Hegemony and Resistance

Is resistance to hegemony possible, and if so, what form must it take to be genuinely counter-hegemonic rather than merely reformist?

---

What distinguishes counter-hegemony from mere opposition?

Is opposition that operates within the terms set by the hegemonic order genuinely counter-hegemonic, or does it reinforce the order by accepting its frame?

Can one use the master's tools to dismantle the master's house, or does the use of hegemonic tools reproduce hegemonic structures?

If the master's tools cannot dismantle the master's house, where do alternative tools come from, given that the master's house is all anyone has known?

Does counter-hegemony require the construction of an alternative common sense, and can alternative common sense be constructed deliberately or does it emerge organically from subaltern experience?

What is the subaltern, and can the subaltern speak?

If the subaltern cannot speak within the dominant discourse, does the effort to give voice to the subaltern inevitably translate subaltern experience into hegemonic categories?

Does translation always betray, or can it preserve something essential while changing the form?

Is the concept of an "essential" subaltern experience itself hegemonic — a romantic projection by intellectuals who need the subaltern to be pure in order to serve as the foundation for critique?

Does the critique of essentialism serve hegemony by dissolving the identities around which resistance could organize?

---

Is a war of position the only viable counter-hegemonic strategy under conditions of advanced capitalism?

What did Gramsci mean by distinguishing a war of position from a war of maneuver, and is this distinction still useful?

Is a war of position a strategy of gradual infiltration, and does infiltration risk assimilation?

Can one infiltrate hegemonic institutions without being transformed by them?

Does the long march through the institutions end in the transformation of the institutions or in the transformation of the marchers?

If the marchers are transformed, does that mean hegemony has won, or that the marchers have become a new hegemonic force from within?

Is entryism a counter-hegemonic strategy or a recipe for co-optation?

Does every successful counter-hegemonic movement eventually become the new hegemony?

If counter-hegemony inevitably becomes hegemony, is the cycle of hegemony and counter-hegemony the fundamental structure of political history?

Is the idea that political history is a cycle of hegemonies a conservative idea — one that forecloses the possibility of genuine liberation by presenting domination as permanent?

---

Can art be counter-hegemonic?
