# Recursive Causal Interrogation: Conflict

## The Clash

What is it that happens when two forces meet and neither yields?

Is the clash a property of the forces themselves, or of the meeting?

Could there be a clash without a point of contact, or does collision require intersection?

Does the clash exist in the moment of impact, or does it persist as long as the forces remain opposed?

Is the persistence of a clash a single sustained event, or a series of micro-collisions continuously renewed?

If the clash is continuously renewed, what sustains the renewal — the forces, or the opposition itself?

Can opposition sustain itself without the forces that oppose, or does opposition require perpetual input?

Does the need for perpetual input mean that every clash is fundamentally unstable, always on the verge of dissolution?

If every clash tends toward dissolution, what prevents certain clashes from dissolving for centuries?

Is the longevity of a clash evidence of something deeper than the surface forces — a structural necessity for the opposition to exist?

---

What distinguishes a clash from a mere difference?

Is it possible for two things to differ without clashing, and if so, what additional element transforms difference into clash?

Is that additional element always force, or can clashes arise from incompatible logics that exert no force at all?

If incompatible logics can clash without force, does this mean conflict is fundamentally conceptual before it is physical?

Can a purely conceptual clash have real consequences, or must it manifest physically to matter?

If conceptual clashes must manifest physically to matter, does this subordinate thought to action, or does thought set the terms of action?

When thought sets the terms of action, does the clash begin in the thinking or in the acting?

If the clash begins in the thinking, can one be in conflict before one has done anything?

Can a person be at war with another solely in their mind, and if so, is this real conflict or merely its anticipation?

Is anticipation of conflict already a form of conflict, given that it organizes perception and behavior around opposition?

---

Does every clash presuppose a space in which the clashing occurs?

What is the nature of this space — is it physical, social, conceptual, or all three simultaneously?

Can a clash create its own space, or must the space pre-exist the conflict?

If the clash creates its own space, does this mean that conflict is generative even in its destructiveness?

Is the generative capacity of conflict parasitic on what it destroys, or does it produce something genuinely new?

If conflict produces something genuinely new, is this production intentional or accidental?

Can unintended consequences of conflict be more significant than the intended outcomes?

If the most significant outcomes of conflict are unintended, does this mean that the agents of conflict never truly control its meaning?

Does the loss of control over meaning transform the clash into something neither party recognizes?

When a clash becomes unrecognizable to its participants, has the original conflict ended and a new one begun, or is it the same conflict in disguise?

---

Is the clash always binary, or can multiple forces clash simultaneously in irreducibly complex configurations?

If clashes can be irreducibly complex, does the binary model of conflict distort our understanding of most real disputes?

Does the binary model persist because it simplifies, or because something about human cognition demands opposition be framed as two-sided?

If human cognition demands binary framing, is every perception of conflict already a reduction of something more complex?

Does this reduction itself cause further conflict, as parties resist being forced into binary categories?

Is the resistance to categorization itself a form of clash — a conflict about how to understand conflict?

Can meta-conflict — conflict about the nature of the conflict — be resolved without first resolving the object-level conflict?

Or does resolving the meta-conflict sometimes dissolve the object-level conflict by reframing it entirely?

If reframing can dissolve conflict, was the original conflict real, or was it an artifact of the frame?

Can a conflict be simultaneously real in its consequences and artificial in its construction?

## What Conflict Is

What is the ontological status of conflict — is it a substance, a relation, a process, or an event?

If conflict is a relation, what are the relata, and can they exist independently of the conflictual relation?

If the relata can exist independently, does conflict supervene on independently existing entities, or does it transform them?

If conflict transforms the entities that enter it, are the parties to a conflict the same entities that existed before the conflict began?

If they are not the same, who exactly is in conflict — the pre-conflict selves or the conflict-transformed selves?

Does the transformation happen at the moment of conflict's onset, or gradually as the conflict unfolds?

If the transformation is gradual, is there a point at which the parties have changed so much that the original conflict no longer applies?

When the original conflict no longer applies, does a new conflict automatically take its place, or is there a gap?

What would it mean for there to be a gap between conflicts — a moment of pure non-opposition?

Is pure non-opposition even conceivable for beings who define themselves partly through what they oppose?

---

Is conflict a natural kind, or is it a concept imposed on diverse phenomena that share only family resemblances?

If conflict is a family resemblance concept, what are the paradigm cases around which the family clusters?

Are paradigm cases of conflict always violent, or can nonviolent opposition serve as an equally central example?

If nonviolent opposition is a paradigm case, does this expand the concept of conflict beyond what is analytically useful?

Where is the boundary between conflict and mere disagreement, and who has the authority to draw it?

Does the authority to define conflict itself become a site of conflict?

If definitions of conflict are contested, can we study conflict without first resolving the definitional dispute?

Is the attempt to define conflict before studying it a form of intellectual imperialism — imposing order on what resists it?

Does conflict resist definition precisely because it is the process by which definitions are contested?

If conflict is the process by which definitions are contested, is defining conflict a performative contradiction?

---

Can conflict exist without awareness — can two parties be in conflict without either knowing it?

If unconscious conflict is possible, what makes it conflict rather than mere structural tension?

Is structural tension always latent conflict, or is there a meaningful distinction between the two?

If there is a distinction, what activates latent conflict — awareness, action, or something else?

Can awareness of structural tension create conflict where none existed, making consciousness itself a catalyst of opposition?

If consciousness catalyzes conflict, is ignorance a form of peace or merely a form of unrecognized war?

Is unrecognized war more dangerous than recognized conflict, because it operates without the constraints that recognition imposes?

Do the constraints that recognition imposes on conflict — rules, norms, laws — transform conflict or merely regulate it?

Can regulation of conflict preserve the conflict indefinitely by preventing it from reaching a resolution?

Is indefinite regulated conflict a stable equilibrium or a slow accumulation of pressure toward eventual rupture?

---

Does conflict require intentionality, or can it arise from purely mechanical processes?

If mechanical processes can produce conflict, does this mean that conflict is more fundamental than consciousness?

If conflict is more fundamental than consciousness, does consciousness emerge from conflict rather than the reverse?

Could consciousness itself be understood as an internalized conflict — a clash between competing perceptual and evaluative processes?

If consciousness is internalized conflict, does the resolution of all internal conflict mean the end of consciousness?

Would a being with no internal conflict be more conscious or less conscious than one riven by contradiction?

Is the productivity of consciousness — its capacity for creation and discovery — dependent on internal conflict?

If creativity requires internal conflict, does the desire for inner peace conflict with the desire for creative vitality?

Is this conflict between peace and creativity itself a productive contradiction, or a genuine dilemma?

Can a genuine dilemma be productive, or does the concept of productivity already assume that dilemmas can be resolved?

## Conflict and Opposition

Is opposition the essence of conflict, or merely one of its features?

Can there be conflict without opposition — a clash in which no party stands against another?

If conflict requires opposition, does it also require that the parties recognize each other as opponents?

Can one be opposed to something without being recognized by it in return?

If opposition can be unilateral, is the opposed party in conflict even if they are unaware of the opposition?

Does unilateral opposition create an asymmetry that itself becomes a source of further conflict?

Is asymmetric conflict fundamentally different from symmetric conflict, or is all conflict asymmetric at some level of description?

If all conflict is asymmetric, does the concept of a fair fight rest on an illusion of symmetry?

Is the illusion of symmetry necessary for the legitimacy of conflict, or can legitimate conflict acknowledge its asymmetries?

Does acknowledging asymmetry in conflict undermine the will to fight, or does it clarify what is actually at stake?

---

Is opposition always relational, or can something be opposed in itself — self-opposed?

If self-opposition is possible, is it the most fundamental form of conflict, from which all external conflict derives?

Does the concept of self-opposition require a divided self, and if so, what divides the self against itself?

Is the self divided by competing desires, incompatible commitments, or irreconcilable aspects of its own identity?

Can the divided self ever achieve unity, or is division constitutive of selfhood?

If division is constitutive of selfhood, is the desire for wholeness a desire to cease being a self?

Does the conflict between self-division and the desire for wholeness drive all human striving?

Is human striving fundamentally an attempt to resolve an irresolvable inner opposition?

If the inner opposition is irresolvable, does every attempt at resolution create new oppositions?

Is the creation of new oppositions through failed resolutions the engine of history, or its tragedy?

---

Does opposition require negation — must one party negate something about the other?

Is negation in conflict always destructive, or can it be a creative act that clears space for new possibilities?

If negation clears space, does it do so by destroying what occupied the space, or by revealing space that was always there?

Is the space revealed by negation a genuine void, or is it already pregnant with potential?

If the space is pregnant with potential, did the negation create the potential or merely uncover it?

Does this question about negation and potential apply to all forms of opposition, or only to dialectical conflict?

What makes a conflict dialectical rather than merely oppositional?

Is dialectical conflict a special case of conflict that tends toward synthesis, or is all conflict implicitly dialectical?

If all conflict is implicitly dialectical, why do some conflicts seem to produce only destruction without synthesis?

Is the appearance of pure destruction an illusion born of temporal perspective — is the synthesis merely not yet visible?

## Conflict and Difference

Does all difference entail conflict, or is difference a necessary but insufficient condition for conflict?

What transforms mere difference into conflict — is it the valuation of difference, the incompatibility of differences, or the scarcity of space for differences to coexist?

If scarcity of space transforms difference into conflict, is conflict ultimately a spatial problem?

Can conflict be resolved by expanding the space available, or does expansion merely defer the conflict to a larger arena?

If expansion defers conflict, does this mean that conflict is not about space at all, but about something that follows difference everywhere?

What follows difference everywhere — is it the impulse to rank, to dominate, to assimilate, or to exclude?

Can difference exist without ranking, or does every recognition of difference automatically introduce a hierarchy?

If recognition of difference automatically introduces hierarchy, is the egalitarian ideal of respecting difference internally contradictory?

Is the contradiction in egalitarianism a productive tension or a fatal flaw?

If it is a productive tension, does this mean that the most just societies are those that sustain conflict about equality rather than resolving it?

---

Is the difference that produces conflict always perceived, or can real differences produce conflict even when unperceived?

If unperceived differences can produce conflict, how does a conflict that no one perceives manifest?

Does it manifest as systemic dysfunction, structural inequality, or a vague sense of unease that lacks an identifiable source?

If conflict manifests as a vague sense of unease, can it be addressed without first making the underlying difference explicit?

Does making the underlying difference explicit intensify the conflict, at least initially?

Is the intensification of conflict through articulation a reason to leave some differences unarticulated?

Is the strategic non-articulation of difference a form of conflict avoidance or a form of conflict management?

Can conflict management without articulation work, or does it merely transform explicit conflict into implicit tension?

Is implicit tension less harmful than explicit conflict, or does it cause different and perhaps deeper forms of damage?

Can the damage caused by implicit tension be recognized and repaired without re-entering the explicit conflict it was designed to avoid?

---

Is the fear of difference the root of all conflict, or is it possible to fear sameness as much as difference?

If sameness can be feared, does the conflict arising from feared sameness differ in kind from the conflict arising from feared difference?

Is the fear of being the same as the other a fear of losing identity, and if so, does conflict serve to maintain distinctions?

If conflict maintains distinctions, is it a mechanism of identity preservation?

Does identity preservation through conflict require a permanent enemy, and if so, does identity become dependent on enmity?

If identity depends on enmity, is the enemy paradoxically essential to the self — more intimate than an ally?

Does the intimacy of enmity explain why former enemies often become the closest allies, and former allies the bitterest enemies?

Is the inversion of enmity and alliance evidence that the content of the relationship matters less than its intensity?

If intensity matters more than content, is conflict simply the name we give to relationships of maximum intensity?

Can maximum intensity be sustained without conflict, or is conflict the only form that maximum relational intensity can take?

## Conflict and Identity

Does conflict constitute identity, or does identity give rise to conflict?

If identity gives rise to conflict, what is it about identity that makes conflict inevitable?

Is it the boundedness of identity — the fact that to be something is to not be something else — that generates opposition?

If boundedness generates opposition, is a boundless identity possible, and would it be free of conflict?

Would a boundless identity still be an identity, or would the absence of boundaries dissolve identity into undifferentiated being?

Is the choice between bounded identity with conflict and boundless non-identity without conflict a genuine choice, or is it forced by the conditions of existence?

If the conditions of existence force bounded identity, is conflict a transcendental condition of selfhood?

If conflict is a transcendental condition of selfhood, can we coherently desire the end of all conflict?

Is the desire to end all conflict a desire for self-dissolution, and if so, is it life-denying?

Can one affirm life while desiring the end of conflict, or does the affirmation of life include the affirmation of conflict?

---

Does conflict reveal identity or create it?

If conflict reveals identity, does this mean identity pre-exists conflict and merely becomes visible through it?

If identity pre-exists conflict, where does it reside before the conflict makes it manifest?

Is latent identity meaningful, or does identity only become real in the moment of its assertion against opposition?

If identity becomes real only through assertion against opposition, do we need opponents in order to know who we are?

Does the need for opponents to know oneself create a perverse incentive to seek out or manufacture conflict?

Is the manufacture of conflict for identity purposes a pathology, or is it an unavoidable feature of self-constitution?

Can self-constitution occur through cooperation rather than conflict, and if so, does the resulting identity differ?

Is identity constituted through cooperation more stable but less vivid than identity constituted through conflict?

Is the vividness of conflict-forged identity a genuine value, or a seductive illusion that glorifies struggle?

---

When groups define themselves through shared conflict, does the conflict become sacred — untouchable by rational analysis?

If conflict becomes sacred, can it be ended without destroying the group identity it sustains?

Does the destruction of group identity through conflict resolution explain why some groups resist peace?

Is the resistance to peace rational from the perspective of identity preservation, even if irrational from the perspective of well-being?

Can identity preservation and well-being be reconciled, or do they sometimes genuinely conflict?

If they genuinely conflict, which takes priority — being who you are or being well?

Is the question of priority itself a conflict between competing values, and if so, how can it be resolved without begging the question?

Does every attempt to resolve the conflict between identity and well-being appeal to a higher identity or a deeper well-being?

Is the appeal to something higher or deeper an infinite regress, or does it terminate in something ultimate?

If it terminates in something ultimate, is that ultimate ground itself constituted by a more fundamental conflict?

---

Can one be in conflict with one's own identity — not just with parts of oneself, but with the very fact of having a particular identity?

Is the conflict with one's own identity a conflict with the limitations that identity imposes?

Are the limitations of identity experienced as external impositions or as internal constraints?

If they are experienced as internal constraints, is the conflict with identity a conflict with oneself at the deepest level?

Can this deepest conflict be resolved by changing one's identity, or does the new identity bring its own constraints and thus its own conflicts?

If every identity brings its own conflicts, is there a form of selfhood that is not identity-based and therefore not conflict-prone?

Would a non-identity-based selfhood be a selfhood at all, or something entirely different — a mode of being rather than a way of being someone?

Is the distinction between being and being someone the most fundamental distinction in the metaphysics of conflict?

Does conflict always presuppose being someone, or can pure being — being without identity — also enter into conflict?

If pure being cannot enter into conflict, is conflict exclusively a feature of the personal, the individual, the identified?

## Conflict and the Other

Is the other always the source of conflict, or can conflict arise without any encounter with alterity?

If conflict requires alterity, does it also require that the other be genuinely other — irreducible to the self?

Can genuine alterity be perceived, or does the self always assimilate the other into its own categories?

If the self always assimilates the other, is every conflict actually a conflict with an image of the other rather than with the other itself?

Does conflict with an image of the other make the conflict less real, or does it reveal that conflict is always imaginary at some level?

If conflict is always imaginary at some level, can it be dissolved by correcting the images?

Does correcting the images require encountering the other as they truly are, and is such an encounter possible?

If encountering the other as they truly are is impossible, is all conflict structurally irresolvable at the level of understanding?

Can conflict be resolved without understanding — through coexistence, forbearance, or sheer exhaustion?

Is resolution without understanding genuine resolution, or merely a ceasefire that preserves the underlying misapprehension?

---

Does the other threaten the self simply by existing, or only by existing in a particular way?

If the other threatens simply by existing, is conflict with the other an ontological necessity rather than a contingent occurrence?

If ontologically necessary, can the necessity be mitigated through ethical orientation, or does ethics merely manage an unresolvable existential threat?

Is the ethical demand to respect the other always in tension with the existential impulse to preserve the self?

Can self-preservation and respect for the other be harmonized, or does one always come at the expense of the other?

If one always comes at the expense of the other, does ethics require self-sacrifice?

Is self-sacrifice the highest ethical response to the other, or does it negate the self that the other needs in order to be recognized?

Does the other need the self to remain intact in order to receive recognition, and if so, does this need limit the ethical demand for self-sacrifice?

Is the limitation of self-sacrifice by the need for mutual recognition a compromise with ethics or a deeper expression of it?

Can mutual recognition function as a resolution to the conflict between self-preservation and ethical demand, or does it merely postpone the reckoning?

---

Is the face of the other, in the Levinasian sense, what arrests conflict or what provokes it?

If the face arrests conflict, why does violence against the other so often target the face?

Does targeting the face attempt to destroy the very thing that calls one to ethical responsibility?

Is the destruction of the face an attempt to eliminate the ethical demand, and if so, does this make violence fundamentally anti-ethical rather than merely unethical?

If violence is anti-ethical, does this place conflict and ethics in absolute opposition, or can some conflicts be ethically demanded?

If some conflicts are ethically demanded, how does one fight ethically without destroying the face of the other?

Is the attempt to fight ethically — to oppose without destroying — the essence of nonviolent resistance?

Does nonviolent resistance succeed precisely because it preserves the face of the other while still engaging in conflict?

Can the preservation of the face during conflict transform the conflict itself?

If conflict can be transformed by preserving the face, is this transformation a resolution or merely a different mode of conflict?

## Conflict and Recognition

Is all conflict ultimately a struggle for recognition?

If all conflict is a struggle for recognition, does recognition end conflict or merely transform it?

When recognition is granted, does the conflict that produced the demand for recognition simply vanish, or does it leave residues?

What form do the residues of recognition-conflict take — resentment, gratitude, suspicion, or something else?

Can recognition ever be fully granted, or is every act of recognition partial and therefore potentially a source of further conflict?

If recognition is always partial, is the struggle for recognition interminable?

Is an interminable struggle sustainable, or does it eventually exhaust the parties involved?

If exhaustion ends the struggle, is the resulting cessation of conflict a form of recognition or its opposite — resignation?

Is resignation a form of peace or a form of defeat?

Can one distinguish between genuine peace and resigned acceptance of unresolved conflict?

---

Does the demand for recognition presuppose that the self lacks something that only the other can provide?

If the self lacks something that only the other can provide, is this lack the fundamental source of conflict?

Is the lack constitutive of the self, or is it an accidental deficit that could, in principle, be filled?

If the lack is constitutive, does filling it destroy the self that the lack constituted?

Does the paradox of constitutive lack mean that successful recognition always comes at the cost of the self that desired it?

If the self that desired recognition is destroyed by receiving it, who enjoys the recognition?

Is the post-recognition self a new entity, born from the conflict, owing its existence to the struggle?

Does the post-recognition self carry the memory of the conflict, and if so, does this memory prevent full enjoyment of the recognition?

Is the memory of conflict a wound that never fully heals, or can it become a source of strength and depth?

Can the memory of conflict be a source of strength without simultaneously being a source of potential re-ignition?

---

Is the Hegelian master-slave dialectic the paradigm of all conflict, or is it a special case?

If it is the paradigm, does all conflict reduce to a struggle over who dominates and who serves?

If all conflict reduces to domination and servitude, is equality the resolution of conflict or its impossibility?

Is equality impossible because every relationship involves some asymmetry, or is equality an ideal that can be approximated even if never fully realized?

If equality can only be approximated, does the gap between ideal and actuality itself become a source of conflict?

Is the conflict generated by the gap between ideal and actuality progressive — does it drive us toward better approximations?

Can progress be distinguished from mere change without invoking a standard that is itself contested?

If the standard of progress is contested, is the conflict over standards more fundamental than the conflict over outcomes?

Does the conflict over standards ever resolve, or does every resolution of one standard-conflict open a new one?

Is the perpetual opening of new standard-conflicts the condition of an open society, and its absence the mark of a closed one?

---

Can recognition be coerced, and if so, does coerced recognition satisfy the demand that motivated the conflict?

If coerced recognition does not satisfy, is voluntary recognition the only genuine form?

Does voluntary recognition require the recognizer to have been free not to recognize, and if so, does the power differential in most conflicts undermine the possibility of genuine recognition?

If power differentials undermine genuine recognition, must power be equalized before recognition can occur?

Is the equalization of power itself achievable only through conflict?

If equalization requires conflict, is this a productive paradox — conflict as the precondition for its own resolution?

Can this paradox be escaped, or is it the permanent condition of social life?

If it is the permanent condition, does acceptance of this condition constitute wisdom or capitulation?

Is the difference between wisdom and capitulation a matter of attitude, or does it depend on what one does next?

Does what one does next inevitably reintroduce conflict, completing the cycle?

## Conflict and Power

Is conflict always about power, or can it be about something that power cannot capture?

If conflict can be about something beyond power, what is that something — truth, beauty, love, justice?

Can truth, beauty, love, or justice be pursued without power, or does their pursuit always require the exercise of power?

If their pursuit requires power, does this mean that every conflict, even one motivated by higher ideals, is at bottom a power struggle?

Does reducing all conflict to power struggle commit a category error, or does it reveal a fundamental truth about the human condition?

If it reveals a fundamental truth, is this truth cynical or realistic?

Is the distinction between cynicism and realism itself a site of conflict?

Can this meta-conflict — the conflict over how to interpret conflict — be resolved, or does it persist as long as conflict itself?

If the meta-conflict persists, does this mean that we can never have a neutral theory of conflict?

Does the impossibility of a neutral theory of conflict mean that every theory of conflict is itself an instrument of power?

---

Does power precede conflict, or does conflict create power?

If conflict creates power, is power the residue of past conflicts, crystallized into structures?

Can crystallized power be dissolved, or does it take new conflict to undo old configurations of power?

If new conflict is needed to undo old power, is revolution the only path to structural change?

Can structural change occur without revolution — through reform, evolution, or gradual transformation?

Is gradual transformation always co-opted by existing power structures, making revolution necessary?

Is the necessity of revolution itself a claim made from within a particular power configuration?

Does the power configuration from which the claim of revolutionary necessity emerges shape the revolution it produces?

Can a revolution escape the power dynamics of the order it overthrows, or does it inevitably reproduce them?

If revolution inevitably reproduces old power dynamics, is fundamental structural change impossible?

---

Is power-over (domination) the only form of power relevant to conflict, or is power-with (cooperation) equally relevant?

If power-with is relevant, can it resolve conflicts that power-over only escalates?

Does power-with require equality among participants, and if so, is it unavailable in situations of significant inequality?

If power-with is unavailable in inequality, do the powerless have any recourse other than power-over — seizing power to redistribute it?

Is the seizure of power by the powerless morally different from the exercise of power by the powerful?

If it is morally different, does this moral difference make any practical difference in how the conflict unfolds?

Can moral asymmetry in conflict be leveraged strategically, and if so, does the strategic use of morality corrupt it?

Is the corruption of morality through strategic use an inevitable feature of conflict, or can moral integrity be maintained even in the midst of power struggles?

What would moral integrity in the midst of power struggle look like — refusal to fight, fighting with restraint, or something else entirely?

Is restraint in conflict a sign of strength or a disadvantage that opponents will exploit?

## Conflict and Violence

Is violence the essence of conflict, or its corruption?

If violence is the corruption of conflict, what is uncorrupted conflict — is it a real possibility or an ideal fiction?

Can conflict exist without any form of violence — physical, structural, symbolic, or psychological?

If symbolic violence is included, is all conflict violent by definition, since all conflict involves symbolic imposition?

If all conflict is violent, does the concept of violence lose its critical edge — its ability to distinguish acceptable from unacceptable conflict?

Does the distinction between acceptable and unacceptable conflict map onto the distinction between nonviolent and violent, or does it cut across this boundary?

Can violence sometimes be more acceptable than nonviolent coercion, and if so, what criterion determines acceptability?

Is the criterion of acceptability itself a product of conflict — a standard imposed by those with the power to define the terms?

If the criteria of acceptability are imposed by power, is every judgment about acceptable conflict a legitimation of a particular power configuration?

Can we escape the circularity of power legitimating its own standards of acceptable conflict?

---

Does violence have a logic of its own that overrides the intentions of those who deploy it?

If violence has its own logic, can it be instrumentalized — used as a tool — or does it always exceed instrumental control?

If violence always exceeds control, is every resort to violence a gamble with unpredictable consequences?

Does the unpredictability of violence counsel against its use in all circumstances, or are there situations where the gamble is warranted?

What makes the gamble warranted — the severity of the injustice, the absence of alternatives, or the probability of success?

Can the probability of success be estimated for violence, or is its outcome fundamentally indeterminate?

If the outcome is indeterminate, does this undermine consequentialist justifications for violence?

Without consequentialist justification, can violence be justified deontologically — as a duty in the face of extreme injustice?

Is a duty to commit violence coherent, or does the concept of duty already presuppose respect for persons that violence violates?

If the concept of duty presupposes respect for persons, is all violence a betrayal of the moral framework that might justify it?

---

Does the experience of violence transform both the perpetrator and the victim in irreversible ways?

If the transformation is irreversible, can the pre-violence relationship ever be restored?

If the pre-violence relationship cannot be restored, is reconciliation after violence a creation of something new rather than a return to something old?

Does the creation of something new after violence require acknowledgment of the violence, or can it proceed through forgetting?

Is forgetting violence a form of violence itself — a second violation that denies the reality of the first?

If forgetting is a second violation, is the obligation to remember violence absolute?

Can the obligation to remember become itself a source of conflict — a weaponization of memory?

Is the weaponization of memory inevitable when memory serves identity, and identity requires enemies?

Can memory serve reconciliation rather than identity, and if so, what form of memory would this require?

Would reconciliatory memory remember differently than identity-preserving memory, or would it remember different things?

## Conflict and Nonviolence

Is nonviolence the absence of violence, or is it a positive force with its own dynamics?

If nonviolence is a positive force, what gives it its power — moral authority, strategic advantage, or something else?

Can moral authority function as a force in conflict, or is it effective only when backed by material power?

If moral authority requires material backing, is purely principled nonviolence naive?

Is the charge of naivete against nonviolence itself a form of ideological conflict — an attempt to delegitimate a threatening alternative?

Does the threat that nonviolence poses to violent systems make it more or less effective than violence in challenging those systems?

If nonviolence is more effective, why is violence so much more common in conflict?

Is the prevalence of violence evidence of its effectiveness, or of something else — habit, emotion, structural incentive?

Can structural incentives for violence be dismantled nonviolently, or does their dismantling itself require force?

Is the use of force to dismantle structures of violence paradoxical, and if so, is the paradox inescapable?

---

Does nonviolence require a particular view of the opponent — as redeemable, as capable of conscience, as human?

If nonviolence requires seeing the opponent as human, does it fail when the opponent refuses to see you as human?

Can nonviolence function asymmetrically — practiced by one side while the other uses violence?

If asymmetric nonviolence can function, what sustains it in the face of violent response — faith, strategy, or community?

Is faith in nonviolence fundamentally religious, or can it be grounded in secular philosophy?

If secular grounds exist, are they as motivationally powerful as religious conviction?

Does the motivational power of a commitment determine its truth, or is truth independent of motivation?

If truth is independent of motivation, can a true commitment to nonviolence be practically impotent?

Is a true but impotent commitment worth holding, or does its impotence refute it?

Does the question of whether impotence refutes a commitment itself reveal a bias toward effectiveness that nonviolence challenges?

---

Can nonviolent conflict be as transformative as violent conflict?

If nonviolent conflict is transformative, does it transform through the same mechanisms as violent conflict — disruption, pressure, crisis?

If the mechanisms are the same, is the distinction between violent and nonviolent conflict less significant than it appears?

If the distinction is less significant, does this undermine the moral case for nonviolence?

Or does the moral case for nonviolence rest not on its difference in mechanism but on its difference in meaning?

What does it mean for a conflict to have a different meaning while employing similar mechanisms?

Is meaning in conflict determined by the intentions of the actors, the experience of the affected, or the judgment of observers?

If meaning is determined differently by actors, affected, and observers, whose determination takes precedence?

Does the precedence question itself become a conflict, and if so, can it be resolved nonviolently?

Is the nonviolent resolution of the conflict over meaning the highest test of nonviolent philosophy?

## Conflict and Peace

Is peace the absence of conflict, or is it a condition that includes conflict in a transformed mode?

If peace includes conflict in a transformed mode, what transformation makes conflict compatible with peace?

Is the transformation a matter of form — how conflict is conducted — or of substance — what the conflict is about?

Can any form of conflict be peaceful if conducted properly, or are some conflicts inherently incompatible with peace?

What makes a conflict inherently incompatible with peace — its stakes, its methods, or its existential depth?

If existential depth makes a conflict incompatible with peace, does this mean that superficial conflicts are easier to pacify?

Is the pacification of superficial conflict genuinely peaceful, or does it merely suppress surface tension while deeper conflicts fester?

If deeper conflicts fester, is apparent peace worse than open conflict because it creates a false sense of security?

Does a false sense of security eventually lead to more catastrophic conflict than would have occurred without it?

Is the catastrophe that follows false peace evidence that genuine peace requires confronting deep conflicts rather than avoiding them?

---

Can peace be imposed, or must it be chosen?
