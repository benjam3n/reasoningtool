# Pure Regress v3

Starting point: "Previous approaches haven't worked."

What caused this assessment?

Someone reports trying many approaches without lasting results.

Do I need to take their word for it?

Is there a way to verify without asking?

I could reason about it. If approaches had worked, would they be seeking an ultimate solution?

Probably not. Seeking an ultimate solution suggests current solutions aren't satisfying.

Is "not satisfying" the same as "not working"?

No. Something could work but not feel satisfying. The bar could be miscalibrated.

Can I tell which it is - not working vs not satisfying?

What evidence would distinguish them?

If approaches objectively failed (no change in measurable outcomes) - that's "not working."
If approaches produced change but the change wasn't recognized or valued - that's "not satisfying."

"Without lasting results" - this suggests some results but not lasting. So something happened but didn't persist.

"No impressive effect" - this is about feeling, not outcome. Something might have worked but it didn't feel impressive.

So there's a mix: some things didn't produce lasting change, some things didn't feel impressive.

Are these the same problem?

No. "Didn't last" is about sustainability. "Didn't feel impressive" is about perception/expectation.

These are different causal chains. They require separate tracing.

---

## Chain 1: "Didn't last"

What would cause results to not last?
- Approach requires ongoing effort that wasn't sustained
- Approach addressed symptom not cause
- Approach worked temporarily then the system adapted
- Approach never actually worked, just seemed to initially
- External factors changed

What would cause stopping an approach?

- Didn't see results fast enough
- Too hard to maintain
- Something else became priority
- Lost motivation
- Decided it wasn't working

If someone stopped because they decided it wasn't working, but it actually was working slowly, then the issue is premature stopping based on wrong assessment.

What would cause wrong assessment?

- No clear metrics
- Wrong metrics
- Timeframe too short
- Comparing to unrealistic standard

This connects to "expectations too high" from the perception side.

## Chain 2: "Didn't feel impressive"

What would cause something to not feel impressive?
- Expectations too high
- Comparing to others' reported results
- Gradual change not noticed
- Looking for wrong markers
- Actually wasn't impressive (small effect)

---

## Convergence: Lack of clear success criteria

Is there a common cause underlying both "didn't last" and "didn't feel impressive"?

Maybe: lack of clear, appropriate success criteria.

What would cause someone to not have clear success criteria?

- Never defined them
- Defined them wrongly (based on others' criteria)
- Criteria keep shifting
- Don't know what realistic criteria would be
- Avoid defining them (keeps options open, avoids failure)

The last one is interesting. Avoiding clear criteria means you can't clearly fail but also can't clearly succeed.

Is that protective? If you can't clearly fail, you don't have to face failure.

But you also can't clearly succeed. So you stay in limbo.

A vague bar - "not easy and not surpassed by others" - can never clearly be met because it's not operationalized. What counts as easy? Who are the others? These are undefined.

What caused this vague bar?

Vague criteria serve a protective function. They prevent definitive failure. But they also prevent definitive success.

---

## The perfectionist loop as abstract pattern

Consider the pattern: keep looking for better solutions even when one works.

If you keep looking for better even when something works, you might abandon working approaches in search of better ones. Then you'd say nothing worked, when actually things worked but were abandoned.

If this is the pattern, what would an "ultimate meta-strategy" represent?

The perfect solution that wouldn't need to be abandoned for something better. The final answer.

Is there such a thing?

Alternative: the "right thing" doesn't exist and the search is the problem.

Which is true?

I can't know in general. But I can question: is the search for an ultimate strategy itself a manifestation of the perfectionist pattern?

If so, then providing an "ultimate strategy" feeds the pattern rather than resolving it.

What would resolve it?

Maybe: recognizing that "ultimate" isn't necessary. Good enough that you actually use beats perfect that you keep searching for.

But there might be legitimate reasons for wanting something foundational - if non-foundational approaches have genuinely caused harm or wasted years, wanting certainty is rational.

Is certainty available?

In some domains, yes. Math, logic, well-understood systems.

In complex, individual-variation domains? Less so.

So the desire for certainty is understandable but might not be satisfiable in the domains where it's most wanted.

What would resolve that tension?

Maybe: certainty about the process even if not about outcomes. Knowing you're using the best available method even if results aren't guaranteed.

What would make a process trustworthy?

- Doesn't assume things without checking
- Questions its own assumptions
- Traces to causes not symptoms
- Derives from your situation not imported
- Survives its own interrogation

---

## The consumption-action gap

A recurring pattern: someone consumes extensive content about a domain but does not take direct action within that domain.

What causes the gap between consuming and acting?

- Content consumption feels like doing something
- Acting requires more activation energy
- Acting makes it real (might fail, might find something bad)
- Uncertainty about what specific action to take
- Waiting for the "right" approach before acting

The last one connects to perfectionism again. If you're looking for the right approach, you might not act until you find it. But you keep consuming content searching for the right approach. Loop.

What breaks the loop?

Either: finding the "right" approach and acting on it.
Or: acting without certainty that it's the "right" approach.

The second is faster but conflicts with the desire for certainty.

Can both be satisfied?

What if investigation itself is clearly "right" as a step, even if the larger strategy is uncertain?

---

## Investigation as logically necessary

Given:
- A problem exists
- The cause of the problem is unknown
- Knowing the cause would inform what to do
- Investigation is how causes are discovered

Then: investigation is necessary to reduce uncertainty about cause.

Are there alternatives to investigation?

- Guess based on pattern matching (frameworks, heuristics)
- Try interventions and observe
- Wait and see if it resolves
- Ask others with similar problems

None of these replace direct investigation. Guessing hasn't produced certainty. Trying things without knowing cause is inefficient. Waiting hasn't resolved anything.

So investigation remains as the path not taken that would provide information the other paths don't.

Is this reasoning valid?

- Problem exists (stated as fact)
- Cause unknown (stated)
- Investigation reveals causes (general principle, usually true)
- Therefore investigation would reveal cause

The logic follows. The conclusion is sound if the premises are true.

---

## Elimination vs construction as reasoning modes

A possible objection to the above: "you shouldn't just eliminate paths."

What does this objection point at?

Two different reasoning modes:

**Elimination:** here are options, cross out the ones that don't work, what remains is the answer.

**Construction:** here's the situation, here's what follows from it, the path builds logically from the premises.

Elimination feels arbitrary - why those options? What if you missed one?

Construction feels necessary - it follows from what's true.

What would construction look like here?

Starting from what IS true (premises), deriving what MUST follow. Not listing possibilities and narrowing, but building forward from established facts.

## Meta-strategy applied to a specific domain

There's a difference between:
- Being told "you should investigate X" (external advice)
- Tracing causes through a meta-process and arriving at "investigation of X is the remaining unexplored path" (derived conclusion)

The content is similar. The epistemic status is different.

The first is prescription. The second is observation about where the chains lead.

If someone rejects the first framing but might accept the second, the difference matters. "The process leads here" is different from "you should do this."

Is that reframing legitimate or manipulative?

It's legitimate if the tracing actually leads there. It would be manipulative if the conclusion was predetermined and the tracing was theater.

Did the tracing actually lead there, or did I know where I wanted to end up?

I can check: were there branch points where it could have gone differently? Yes - multiple chains were followed, and they converged on the same point independently. That convergence suggests the conclusion is derived, not predetermined.

## The knowing-doing gap as core pattern

The gap keeps appearing:
- Framework knowledge exists but isn't applied to specific problems
- Analytical skill exists but isn't applied to achieve concrete goals
- Meta-strategy is being developed but hasn't produced concrete change

What is the gap made of?

The gap is between knowing/having and doing/using.

What fills the space between knowing and doing?

- Decision to act
- Activation energy to start
- Belief that action will work
- Clarity about specific action to take
- Nothing external blocking

Which of these is typically missing?

"Uncertainty about whether it's the right action" - connects to wanting certainty before acting.

"Waiting for better option" - connects to perfectionism.

These keep appearing. Perfectionism and desire for certainty as blockers to applying what is already known.

Are they the root cause? Or are they also symptoms of something deeper?

For the purpose of finding a path forward, this may not matter. What matters is identifying what action would move things and what's blocking it. If the block can be dissolved without resolving its deeper causes, that's sufficient.

If the action is logically necessary (as argued above for investigation), the block might not apply - because the question "is this the right action?" has been answered by the derivation.

---

## What emerged from this tracing

1. "Didn't last" (sustainability problem) and "didn't feel impressive" (perception/expectation problem) are different causal chains that are often conflated. Separating them reveals different things to address.

2. Lack of clear success criteria is a common underlying cause of perceived failure across both chains.

3. Vague criteria serve a protective function - they prevent clear failure but also prevent clear success. This creates limbo.

4. The perfectionist loop as abstract pattern: keep searching for better even when something works, abandoning working approaches, then concluding nothing works.

5. The consumption-action gap: consuming content about a domain feels like doing something, substituting for direct action. This creates a loop when combined with waiting for the "right" approach.

6. Investigation is logically necessary given: problem exists + cause unknown + investigation reveals causes. This is a deductive argument, not a recommendation.

7. Elimination (ruling out options) and construction (building from premises) are different reasoning modes. Construction feels more necessary and less arbitrary because conclusions follow from what's established rather than from what's been crossed off.

8. A meta-strategy applied to a specific domain produces different epistemic status than being told what to do. "The process leads here" is different from "you should do this." The tracing must be genuine, not theater for a predetermined conclusion.

9. The knowing-doing gap is the recurring core pattern: framework knowledge exists, analytical capability exists, but neither is applied to produce concrete change. Perfectionism and desire for certainty appear as the primary blockers in this gap.

These are observations from the tracing, not prescriptions.
