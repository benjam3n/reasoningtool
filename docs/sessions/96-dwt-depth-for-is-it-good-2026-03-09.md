## Section C: Completeness Check

### Step C1: What are the options?

The "decision" here isn't between competing choices — it's about depth of analysis for answering the question. Reframing:

- **Option 1: Shallow** — 1-3 sentences, gut-level ("yes because I find it useful")
- **Option 2: Moderate** — 1-2 pages, identify criteria, score against them, note gaps (~1-4 hours)
- **Option 3: Deep** — Structured multi-criteria evaluation with evidence, comparisons to alternatives, user testing data, longitudinal assessment (~weeks)

### Step C2: Evaluation criteria for choosing depth

What makes one depth better than another?

1. **Stakes**: What rides on getting this answer right?
2. **Reversibility**: If I act on a wrong answer, can I course-correct easily?
3. **Decision lifespan**: How long will I live with whatever conclusion I reach?
4. **Information availability**: Is the information needed for deeper analysis actually obtainable?
5. **Audience**: Who is the answer for, and what do they need?
6. **Marginal value of more depth**: Does going deeper actually change the conclusion?

### Step C3: Scoring each depth option

| Criterion | Shallow (gut) | Moderate (structured) | Deep (formal evaluation) |
|-----------|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Stakes | GOOD (if personal/low-stakes) | GOOD (covers most contexts) | GOOD (only if high stakes demand it) |
| Reversibility | GOOD (opinion can change anytime) | GOOD (same) | GOOD (same) |
| Decision lifespan | OK (fine for today's mood) | GOOD (holds up for months) | OK (overkill — may be stale before it's finished) |
| Information availability | GOOD (gut is always available) | GOOD (criteria are articulable) | BAD (user testing data, longitudinal evidence probably doesn't exist yet) |
| Audience fit | OK (fine for self, weak for others) | GOOD (persuasive for most audiences) | OK (only needed for investors/formal stakeholders) |
| Marginal value of depth | BAD (too shallow to surface non-obvious insights) | GOOD (sweet spot — surfaces real structure) | BAD (diminishing returns — deeper analysis unlikely to flip the conclusion) |

### Step C4: Is there a clear winner?

**Yes. Moderate depth is the clear winner.** It scores GOOD on 5 of 6 criteria and has no BADs. The other two options each have at least one BAD.

**Decision reached at Step C4. Per the procedure: That is the answer.**

## Applying the Time Proportioning Rule (Card 3)

The question "is reasoningtool good?" has a decision lifespan of roughly **1-6 months** — the assessment might inform what to work on next, how to describe it to others, or whether to keep investing time. It's not permanent. It will get reassessed naturally as the project evolves.

| Decision lifespan | Maximum analysis time |
|-------------------|-----------------------|
| 1 month | 3 hours |
| 6 months | 1-2 days |

**Warranted analysis time: 1-4 hours maximum.** Enough for a structured evaluation. Not enough for (nor would it benefit from) a multi-week formal assessment.

---

## The Verdict: What Depth Is Warranted

**A moderate, structured analysis of 1-4 hours.** Specifically:

1. **Define "good" with 4-6 explicit criteria** (e.g., does it produce better thinking outputs? Is it usable? Does it cover the right territory? Is it differentiated from alternatives? Does it actually get used?)
2. **Score the tool against those criteria** using GOOD/OK/BAD with brief evidence for each
3. **Identify 1-3 genuine unknowns** that would require user feedback or comparative testing to resolve
4. **State a provisional conclusion** you're willing to act on

What is **not warranted**:

- **Less than this**: A gut "yes it's good" has the problem that the creator built it, so the gut is biased. Even 30 minutes of structured criteria-checking will surface something a gut reaction misses.
- **More than this**: A formal multi-week evaluation is not warranted because (a) the information for deep analysis (user testing data, longitudinal outcomes) probably doesn't exist yet, (b) the project is still evolving so any deep snapshot will be stale quickly, and (c) the decision is fully reversible — if the conclusion is "it's good" and evidence later says otherwise, course-correction is easy.

## The Three Questions Test (Card 4 — fastest check)

1. *"If I had to answer this instant, do I know what I'd say?"* — Probably yes ("yes, it's good, but..."). The "but" suggests the gut answer exists but isn't fully trusted.
2. *"Is there one specific thing I could learn that would change my answer?"* — **Yes: whether someone other than the creator finds it useful.** That's the key unknown.
3. *"Am I going in circles?"* — Not yet, because the analysis hasn't been done at all.

**Stop at Question 2.** The one thing to investigate: get a non-creator to use it on a real problem and observe what happens. That single data point is worth more than 100 hours of self-evaluation.

## Common Mistakes to Watch For

- **Mistake 2 (Research as Procrastination)**: Don't spend 4 hours reading about evaluation frameworks. Spend 4 hours *evaluating*.
- **Mistake 3 (Waiting for Certainty)**: "Is it good?" will never have a 95%-confident answer. Act at 70%.
- **Mistake 5 (Optimizing When You Should Be Satisficing)**: The question only needs a satisficing answer — "good enough to keep investing in" or "not good enough, redirect effort." It does not need a precise quality score.
