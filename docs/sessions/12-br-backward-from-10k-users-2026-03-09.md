# /br Working backward from "10,000 people use reasoningtool weekly" — what had to happen?
**Date:** 2026-03-09
**Skill:** /br (Backward Reasoning)

---

**Interpretation selected:** Interpretation 2 — Goal-backward planning. You have a desired end state (10,000 weekly users) and want to work backward to figure out what had to be in place.

**Depth:** 2x (5 backward steps, 2 premise chains, 2 alternative paths, 2 validation checks)

---

## Step 1: The Endpoint

**endpoint_statement:** 10,000 people use reasoningtool weekly. They open Claude Code, invoke skills from the plugin, and it is part of their regular workflow. This is happening consistently, not a one-time spike.

What this means concretely: 10,000 distinct humans, each week, type a slash command or otherwise trigger a reasoningtool skill inside Claude Code. They do this because it helps them think better, and they come back because the value compounds.

---

## Step 2: What Goal Does This Serve?

**immediate_purpose:** The endpoint serves the goal of *proving that structured reasoning tools have real demand* — that this isn't a hobby project but a product people rely on. 10K weekly is the threshold where you have a genuine community, word-of-mouth is self-sustaining, and the project's survival no longer depends on the solo developer's willpower alone.

Deeper: it serves the developer's goal of making reasoning accessible — turning the toolkit from "a thing I built" into "a thing that exists in the world and matters."

---

## Step 3: What Problem Generated That Goal?

**generating_problem:** The problem is that structured thinking procedures are trapped. They exist in books, in training programs, in expensive consulting frameworks — but they aren't available at the moment of thought. When someone is actually stuck, they don't reach for a reasoning framework. They muddle through, or they ask an LLM and get generic advice.

The secondary problem: the developer has built 592 skills and nobody is using them. The gap between "this exists" and "this is used" is the central problem.

---

## Step 4: What Context Created That Problem?

**generating_context:** Two contextual forces:

1. **Claude Code plugins are new.** The distribution channel barely exists. There's no App Store equivalent. Discovery is near-zero — people don't browse for Claude Code plugins the way they browse VS Code extensions. The ecosystem is embryonic.

2. **Solo developer with no marketing infrastructure.** No audience, no mailing list, no brand recognition. The project has a website but no distribution engine. Building 592 skills is a manufacturing achievement; reaching 10,000 users is a distribution achievement. These require completely different capabilities.

---

## Step 5: Full Backward Trace

Working backward from 10,000 weekly users, here are the necessary prior states:

### Backward Step 5 (closest to endpoint): Retention is strong
At 10K weekly, retention must be working. People who try it come back. This means the first experience is good enough that users form a habit. The skills must solve real problems, and the interaction pattern (typing a slash command, getting structured output) must feel better than the alternative (just prompting Claude directly).

**What had to be true:** A meaningful subset of the 592 skills are genuinely better than raw prompting. The user can find the right skill without memorizing 592 names. The output format is useful, not just impressive.

### Backward Step 4: Thousands are trying it
Before 10K weekly users, there had to be a period where tens of thousands tried it. At typical retention rates for developer tools (20-40% weekly retention for something good), you need 25K-50K people to have installed and tried it. That's a lot of people who heard about it and took action.

**What had to be true:** The project reached mass awareness in a relevant community. Not "went viral on Twitter for a day" but sustained visibility among people who use Claude Code regularly.

### Backward Step 3: A distribution channel is working
Before thousands tried it, a reliable channel had to exist. This could be:
- Claude Code's own plugin/extension marketplace (if one exists by then)
- A viral loop (users share outputs, others ask "how did you make that?")
- Content marketing that consistently reaches the target audience
- A partnership or integration with Anthropic's ecosystem

**What had to be true:** At least one channel reliably delivers hundreds of new users per week.

### Backward Step 2: The product has a clear entry point
Before any channel works, the product needs a simple pitch and a fast first experience. "592 skills" is not a pitch — it's a feature count. Someone had to figure out the answer to: "What do I say in one sentence that makes a Claude Code user install this in 30 seconds?"

**What had to be true:** There's a killer use case — probably 3-5 skills, not 592 — that demonstrates undeniable value. The "aha moment" is clear and fast. Installation is one command. First skill invocation happens within 60 seconds.

### Backward Step 1 (earliest): The developer shifted from building to distributing
Before everything above, the solo developer had to make a fundamental shift: stop building new skills and start getting existing skills into people's hands. This is the hardest step because building is comfortable and distributing is uncomfortable. The developer had to accept that 592 skills with 0 users is less valuable than 20 skills with 10,000 users.

**What had to be true:** The developer decided that distribution is the bottleneck, not capability. They allocated the majority of their time to reaching people rather than creating more procedures.

## Step 6: The Forward Story (Reconstructed)

### Chapter 1: Foundational Value
A solo developer believes that structured reasoning — the kind found in decision science, systems thinking, epistemology — should be available to anyone at the moment they need it, not locked in books or training programs. They build 592 skills as a Claude Code plugin.

### Chapter 2: The Pivot
The developer recognizes that 592 skills and 0 users means the project is a library, not a product. They make the hard decision to stop building and start distributing. They set a target: 10,000 weekly users.

### Chapter 3: Finding the Wedge
The developer identifies 3-5 "hero skills" — the ones that make people say "I can't go back to not having this." Likely candidates: `/decide` for decisions, `/claim` for evaluating claims, `/diagnose` for debugging problems, `/br` for backward reasoning, `/rca` for root cause analysis. They build the pitch around these, not around "592 skills."

The one-liner becomes something like: *"Think better with Claude. 5 slash commands that replace an hour of muddling."*

### Chapter 4: First 100 Users
The developer goes to where Claude Code users already are — likely forums, Discord servers, Twitter/X, Hacker News, Reddit r/ClaudeAI. They don't post "check out my project." They post examples of the skills in action — showing a `/decide` output on a real decision, a `/br` trace on a real problem. The content IS the product demo.

Some of these land. Early adopters install it. The developer talks to every single one of them. What worked? What confused you? Which skill did you use again?

### Chapter 5: The Channel Clicks
One of these things works at scale:
- **(Path A) Marketplace/ecosystem:** Anthropic launches a plugin directory or Claude Code extensions marketplace. Reasoningtool is well-positioned because it already exists and has polish. Discovery becomes passive.
- **(Path B) Viral loop:** Users start sharing skill outputs in their own contexts — Slack messages, blog posts, tweets. Other people see structured reasoning output and ask where it came from. The output itself becomes the marketing.

The developer doubles down on whichever channel is working. They optimize the first-run experience so that every new install leads to a successful first skill invocation within 60 seconds.

### Chapter 6: Retention Compounds
Users who try 1 skill discover others. The `/meta` skill helps them navigate. Power users find skills like `/stc` (steel-man counterarguments), `/pbr` (probabilistic reasoning), `/se` (solution exploration). The breadth — 592 skills — becomes an asset once someone is already inside, even though it was a liability for first impressions.

Weekly usage stabilizes. Users come back because the skills become how they think through hard problems.

### Chapter 7: 10,000 Weekly Users
The flywheel is turning. New users arrive from the working channel. Retention holds because the core skills are genuinely useful. The developer is now maintaining and improving rather than desperately growing. The project has proven that structured reasoning tools have real demand.

---

## Step 7: Story Evaluation

### Coherence Check 1: Is the path plausible?
The story coheres **if** one critical assumption holds: that structured reasoning via slash commands is meaningfully better than just asking Claude directly. If a user can type "help me think through this decision" and get 80% of the value of `/decide`, the wedge doesn't exist. The entire path depends on the skills providing something that raw prompting does not — structure, rigor, a procedure that catches things the user would miss.

**Verdict:** This is testable right now. Take 10 decisions. Run them through `/decide` and through raw Claude prompting. If the skill output is noticeably better, the wedge exists. If not, the skills need to be reworked before distribution matters.

### Coherence Check 2: Can a solo developer execute this?
The path requires the developer to do marketing, community management, user research, and product optimization — all while maintaining 592 skills. This is a lot for one person.

**Verdict:** Partially coherent. The path is plausible if the developer ruthlessly prioritizes. The key move is treating 90% of the 592 skills as "long tail" that doesn't need active maintenance, and focusing energy on 5-10 hero skills plus one distribution channel. A solo developer cannot do everything; they can do one thing well.

### Alternative Path A: Partnership
Instead of organic growth, the developer partners with Anthropic directly. If reasoningtool demonstrates enough quality, it could become a featured plugin, an example project, or even integrated into Claude Code's default experience. This skips the "find a channel" problem entirely.

**Plausibility:** Low-to-medium. Anthropic has its own priorities. But the project's scope (592 skills) and structure is unusual enough that it might get attention if presented well.

### Alternative Path B: Niche dominance first
Instead of 10K general users, the developer targets one specific community — say, product managers making decisions, or founders evaluating startup ideas — and becomes the known tool for that group. 10K weekly from one niche is harder, but 2K weekly from a niche that talks to each other is achievable and can expand.

**Plausibility:** High. This is how most developer tools grow. The "who is this for" question is more important than the "how do we reach everyone" question.

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Developer shifts from building to distributing | Not yet — still building (592 skills, 0 users) |
| 2 | A clear wedge: 3-5 hero skills with an obvious pitch | Undefined — the pitch is still "592 skills" |
| 3 | A working distribution channel delivers hundreds of new users/week | No channel exists yet |
| 4 | First-run experience converts installs to active users in 60 seconds | Unknown — not tested with real users |
| 5 | Retention holds because skills are genuinely better than raw prompting | Unvalidated assumption |

**The single most important next step:** Validate assumption #5. If the skills aren't meaningfully better than just talking to Claude, nothing else matters. If they are, everything else is solvable.
