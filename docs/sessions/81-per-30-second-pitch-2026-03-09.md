# /per How do I effectively communicate what reasoningtool is to someone in 30 seconds?
**Date:** 2026-03-09
**Skill:** /per (Persuasive Communication)

---

## Step 1: Identify the Audience and Their Current Position

```
AUDIENCE: People who already use AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT, etc.) — likely technically comfortable, may be developers, knowledge workers, or AI enthusiasts
CURRENT POSITION: "I already get good results from AI by writing my own prompts. I don't need a plugin for that."
BELIEF STRENGTH: Casual — they haven't thought deeply about structured thinking procedures; they just have a default habit of prompting freeform
KEY VALUES: Efficiency, getting better output from AI, not wasting time on tools that add friction, being smart/capable
TRUST LEVEL: Low-to-moderate — you're a solo developer they've never heard of, so credibility must be earned in the pitch itself
```

They're not hostile to the idea — they just don't yet see the gap between "asking AI stuff" and "using AI to think systematically." The pitch needs to make that gap visible in seconds.

---

## Step 2: Define the Desired Shift

```
DESIRED OUTCOME: They should think "oh, that's interesting — I want to see what that looks like" and either visit the site or install the plugin
SHIFT DISTANCE: Small — not asking them to change beliefs, just to be curious enough to try
REALISTIC TARGET: Get them to look at it. That's it. 30 seconds cannot convince anyone of deep value — it can only open a door.
```

The realistic win here is not "they understand reasoningtool." It's "they want to understand reasoningtool." Curiosity, not comprehension.

---

## Step 3: Choose Persuasion Approach

```
PRIMARY APPROACH: Narrative — because in 30 seconds, a concrete example lands faster than an abstract description. "Here's what happens when you use it" beats "here's what it is."
SECONDARY APPROACH: Social proof / credibility through specificity — because "592 structured thinking skills" is a concrete, impressive number that signals depth and seriousness without requiring external validation.
```

Logical argument is wrong for 30 seconds — no time. Emotional appeal risks sounding vague. A tight concrete example plus a striking specific detail is the right combo.

---

## Step 4: Structure the Argument

```
STRUCTURE:
1. OPEN: Name the familiar frustration (meet them where they are)
2. BRIDGE: Connect that frustration to the structural gap reasoningtool fills
3. POINT 1: What it is (one sentence, concrete)
4. POINT 2: What it does differently (one example)
5. ACTION: Try it / look at it
```

### The 30-Second Pitch (Version A — Problem-First)

> "You know how when you ask Claude to help you think through a decision, you get a decent answer — but you're never sure if it actually considered the right things? Reasoningtool is 592 structured thinking procedures as a Claude Code plugin. So instead of hoping Claude thinks well, you type `/decide` and it runs a real decision analysis — breaks down your options, surfaces what you're not seeing, checks for bias. It's like giving Claude an actual methodology instead of just vibes."

### The 30-Second Pitch (Version B — Demo-First)

> "So I built this Claude Code plugin — 592 thinking skills. Here's what I mean: you type `/rca` and instead of Claude just guessing at why something went wrong, it runs a structured root cause analysis. Five Whys, causal chains, the whole thing. There's one for decisions, one for assumptions, one for breaking down problems — basically every rigorous thinking method, packaged as a command. It turns Claude from a chatbot into a thinking partner that actually has process."

### The 30-Second Pitch (Version C — Shortest / Punchline)

> "AI is good at generating answers. It's bad at knowing how to think about your problem. Reasoningtool is 592 structured thinking procedures as a Claude Code plugin — root cause analysis, decision frameworks, assumption checks, all of it. You type a command, it runs real methodology. It's the difference between asking Claude to think and giving Claude a way to think."

---

## Step 5: Anticipate and Address Objections

```
OBJECTIONS:

1. "Can't I just prompt Claude to do that myself?"
   Response: You can, and for simple cases that works. But a good root cause analysis has 6 steps that build on each other. A good decision framework checks for 4 types of bias. You'd have to know the methodology to prompt for it — and if you knew it that well, you'd still want it automated so you don't skip steps under time pressure.
   Handle: PROACTIVE — Version A addresses this with "instead of hoping Claude thinks well." Version C addresses it with "the difference between asking Claude to think and giving Claude a way to think."

2. "592 skills? That sounds overwhelming / bloated."
   Response: You don't browse 592 skills. You type `/decide` and the router picks the right ones. It's like a library — the value isn't reading every book, it's that the right book exists when you need it.
   Handle: RESERVE — Don't bring this up in 30 seconds. If they raise it, the library analogy handles it.

3. "I've never heard of this / why should I trust a solo developer's plugin?"
   Response: It's open. You can read every skill — they're just markdown files with structured procedures. The quality is visible in 60 seconds of use.
   Handle: RESERVE — The pitch earns credibility through specificity (592 is a real number, the examples are concrete). Don't defend credibility preemptively in a 30-second window.

4. "Does this actually work better than vanilla Claude?"
   Response: Try `/rca` on a real problem you had last week and compare it to what you'd get from a plain prompt. The difference is structural — it doesn't just answer, it works through the problem in steps.
   Handle: RESERVE — this is handled by the call to action (just try it).
```

## Recommendations

**Use Version C** as your default. It's the tightest, has the strongest punchline ("the difference between asking Claude to think and giving Claude a way to think"), and works in text or speech.

**Use Version B** when you can show a screen or are talking to a developer — the concrete `/rca` example lands harder with people who think in commands.

**Use Version A** when you're talking to someone who's had the specific frustration of getting "decent but shallow" answers from AI.

**Key principles for all versions:**
- Lead with the gap, not the product. The listener needs to feel the problem before they care about the solution.
- The number 592 does real work. It signals this isn't a toy — don't round it or soften it.
- End on the contrast/punchline, not the feature list. The last thing they hear is what they remember.
- The call to action is always "try it" or "look at it" — never "buy it" or "believe me." Low-commitment asks match 30-second interactions.
