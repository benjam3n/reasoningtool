# /argd Build an argument for: "Most people's thinking failures are procedural, not intellectual"
**Date:** 2026-03-09
**Skill:** /argd (Argumentative Document)

---

## Step 1: Define the Thesis

**Thesis:** Most thinking failures in everyday reasoning — poor decisions, missed insights, flawed analyses — result from skipping or missequencing cognitive steps, not from insufficient intelligence or knowledge.

**Distinguishing the thesis:**
- This is not a *topic* ("thinking failures") — it takes a position on their cause.
- This is not a *question* ("why do people think badly?") — it proposes an answer.
- This is not merely an *opinion* ("people should think more carefully") — it makes a falsifiable empirical claim about the structure of failure.

**Falsifiability test:** Someone could disagree by arguing that thinking failures are primarily caused by low fluid intelligence, lack of domain knowledge, or inherent cognitive limitations that no procedure could overcome. That is a real, defensible counter-position.

**Scope:**
- **Covers:** Everyday reasoning, professional judgment, decision-making, problem-solving, and analytical tasks performed by adults of average or above-average intelligence.
- **Does NOT cover:** Clinical cognitive impairments, reasoning by very young children, or tasks that genuinely require rare levels of specialized expertise (e.g., proving novel mathematical theorems).

**Stakes:** If this thesis is correct, the implication is transformative: the primary intervention for better thinking is not education (more knowledge), not selection (smarter people), but *process* — giving people structured procedures and making sure they follow them. This reframes thinking improvement from an aptitude problem to an engineering problem.

---

## Step 2: Identify the Audience

**Primary audience:** Intellectually curious professionals, educators, tool-builders, and anyone skeptical of "thinking frameworks" who assumes that smart people don't need checklists.

**What they currently believe:** Most likely a mixed model — that thinking quality is some combination of intelligence, knowledge, effort, and maybe process. Many implicitly weight intelligence most heavily ("she's just smarter than me"). The sophisticated version of their belief is that procedure might help at the margins, but the heavy lifting is done by raw cognitive ability.

**What would convince them:**
- Empirical evidence that procedure-following predicts outcomes better than ability measures
- Demonstrations that intelligent people fail in characteristic, procedural ways
- Examples where adding procedure dramatically improves results without changing the people involved

**Likely objections:**
- "If it were just about steps, everyone who reads a checklist would think well"
- "You're ignoring the role of intelligence, creativity, and insight"
- "Some problems genuinely require brilliance, not checklists"
- "This is just dressed-up behaviorism"

**Shared premises we can build on:**
- Thinking can go wrong
- Some thinking failures are avoidable in hindsight
- Procedures help in other domains (medicine, aviation)
- Intelligence alone doesn't guarantee good outcomes

## Step 3: Map the Argument Structure

### Supporting Arguments (ordered to build gradually):

**Argument 1: The error pattern is procedural, not intellectual.**
When we examine actual thinking failures, they cluster around *skipped steps* (not considering alternatives, not checking assumptions, not looking for disconfirming evidence) rather than *inability to perform* those steps when prompted. The failure mode is omission, not incapacity.

**Argument 2: Intelligence does not protect against procedural failures.**
Highly intelligent people make the same structural errors as everyone else — often more confidently. IQ and education correlate poorly with resistance to well-documented reasoning biases. If failures were intellectual, smarter people would fail less often on the same tasks. They don't, reliably.

**Argument 3: Adding procedure dramatically improves outcomes without changing the people.**
Checklists, structured analytic techniques, and decision frameworks have repeatedly shown large effect sizes in domains from surgery to intelligence analysis. The same people, with the same brains, produce markedly better results when given procedures. This is the signature of a procedural problem, not an intellectual one.

**Argument 4: The phenomenology of failure confirms it.**
When people see their own errors after the fact, the overwhelming response is not "I couldn't have figured that out" but "I should have thought of that" or "I knew that, I just didn't think to check." The knowledge and capability were present; the process to activate them was absent.

**Argument 5: Evolutionary and architectural arguments predict procedural failure.**
Human working memory is severely limited (roughly 4 chunks). Complex reasoning requires holding many considerations simultaneously. Without external structure, steps get dropped not because of stupidity but because of architectural bottlenecks that affect everyone. Procedure is working-memory augmentation.

**Dependencies:** Arguments 1 and 2 establish the negative case (it's not intelligence). Arguments 3 and 5 establish the positive case (it is procedure). Argument 4 provides phenomenological confirmation. Argument 2 depends partly on Argument 1's framing.

## Step 4: Gather and Assess Evidence

### Argument 1: Error patterns are procedural

**Evidence:**
- Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases research (1970s-present) consistently shows that reasoning errors stem from *not activating* the right cognitive operation — not from inability to perform it. When subjects are prompted to "consider the opposite," accuracy rises dramatically. The operation was available; the trigger was missing.
- Gary Klein's research on naturalistic decision-making shows that expert errors in high-stakes domains (firefighting, military command) are overwhelmingly errors of *situation assessment* — failing to check a critical variable — not errors of reasoning capacity.
- Post-mortem analyses of business failures (e.g., Finkelstein's "Why Smart Executives Fail") consistently find that decision-makers had access to the relevant information and possessed the analytical ability to process it. They failed to look, not to see.

**Strength:** Strong. Multiple independent research traditions converge on the same finding. However, the evidence is stronger for certain error types (judgment under uncertainty, diagnostic reasoning) than others (creative problem-solving).

**Alternative interpretation:** One could argue these studies show that people lack *metacognitive knowledge* — knowing when to deploy which strategy — and that this is a form of intellectual deficit. This is addressed in counterarguments.

### Argument 2: Intelligence doesn't protect

**Evidence:**
- Stanovich's extensive work on "dysrationalia" (2009, 2011) demonstrates that IQ correlates poorly with rational thinking. High-IQ individuals are susceptible to the same biases: base-rate neglect, myside bias, framing effects. Stanovich specifically argues this is because rationality requires *initiating* override procedures, not just having the capacity for them.
- West, Meserve, and Stanovich (2012) found that cognitive ability (SAT scores) did not reduce susceptibility to many biases — and in some cases, smarter individuals were *better* at constructing justifications for their biased conclusions (motivated reasoning).
- The "bias blind spot" finding (Pronin et al., 2002): more intelligent individuals are no better at detecting bias in themselves, though they are slightly better at detecting it in others. The asymmetry suggests a procedural deficit (failure to apply the check reflexively) not an intellectual one.
- Tetlock's forecasting research: untrained experts with high credentials performed poorly; trained forecasters using structured procedures (the "Good Judgment Project") outperformed intelligence analysts with access to classified information. The variable was method, not ability.

**Strength:** Very strong. This is one of the most replicated findings in the judgment and decision-making literature. The Stanovich line of research is particularly compelling because it directly measures the intelligence-rationality gap.

**Could this be interpreted differently?** One could claim that "knowing when to deploy a procedure" is itself an intellectual skill. This is a fair point but actually supports the thesis: if the bottleneck is deployment knowledge (a procedural issue) rather than computational capacity (an intellectual issue), then the fix is to externalize the deployment — which is exactly what structured procedures do.

### Argument 3: Procedure fixes the problem

**Evidence:**
- Gawande's "The Checklist Manifesto" (2009) documents that surgical checklists reduced complications by 36% and deaths by 47% in a WHO study across eight hospitals. The surgeons did not become more skilled; they became more procedural.
- Structured Analytic Techniques (SATs) in intelligence analysis: The U.S. Intelligence Community adopted procedures like Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) after the Iraq WMD failure. Richards Heuer's work demonstrates that analysts using structured procedures generate more hypotheses, consider more evidence, and reach more calibrated conclusions.
- Kahneman's "mediating assessments protocol" for hiring: breaking evaluation into independent, structured assessments of specific traits dramatically outperforms unstructured interviews — even when the interviewers in unstructured settings are experienced and intelligent.
- Pre-mortem technique (Klein, 2007): simply asking "imagine this has failed — why?" before committing to a plan surfaces risks that intelligent, motivated teams consistently miss in standard planning. The thinking capacity existed; the procedural prompt was absent.
- The dramatic success of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), which is essentially a set of thinking procedures (identify the thought, test the evidence, consider alternatives) applied to emotional reasoning. CBT works not by making people smarter but by giving them steps.

**Strength:** Very strong, and this is where the argument becomes most compelling. The effect sizes are large, the domains are diverse, and the mechanism is clear: same people + procedure = better outcomes.

**Cherry-picking check:** Are there cases where adding procedure doesn't help? Yes — in domains requiring genuine creative insight (certain aspects of scientific discovery, artistic creation), procedures seem to help less. This is acknowledged in scope limitations. There are also cases where poorly designed procedures add bureaucratic overhead without improving thinking. The claim is about *well-designed* procedures addressing *characteristic* failures.

### Argument 4: The phenomenology of "I should have thought of that"

**Evidence:**
- This is primarily experiential and introspective, which makes it weaker as formal evidence but powerful as a shared-premise argument. Almost everyone has had the experience of recognizing, in hindsight, that they had the knowledge and ability to reach a better conclusion but simply didn't activate the right considerations.
- Fischhoff's "hindsight bias" research (1975) is relevant here: people reliably feel that outcomes were foreseeable after the fact. While this is partly a bias, it also captures something real — in many cases, the relevant information and reasoning capacity genuinely were available ex ante.
- "Near-miss" analyses in accident investigation (Reason, 1990) consistently find that the information needed to prevent failures existed in the system. People had the data and the intelligence. What was missing was the procedure that would have connected them.

**Strength:** Moderate as standalone evidence, strong as corroboration of the other arguments.

### Argument 5: Cognitive architecture predicts procedural failure

**Evidence:**
- Cowan (2001) established the ~4-item working memory limit. Complex reasoning routinely requires tracking more considerations than this. Without external scaffolding, items get dropped — not because of stupidity but because of architectural constraints that affect everyone.
- Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory (1988, extensive subsequent work): when intrinsic cognitive load exceeds working memory capacity, performance degrades. Procedures reduce extraneous load by offloading sequencing and tracking to external structure.
- The entire field of distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995) demonstrates that complex reasoning in real-world settings always relies on external structures — checklists, instruments, shared representations. The idea that good thinking happens "in the head" without procedural support is itself a misconception.

**Strength:** Strong theoretical support. This doesn't directly prove the thesis but explains *why* procedural failures should be the dominant failure mode — they're predicted by the architecture.

## Step 5: Address Counterarguments

### Counterargument 1: "If it were just procedure, anyone with a checklist would think well"

**Steelmanned version:** Thinking procedures require judgment to apply. You need to know which procedure to use, when to deviate, and how to interpret results. This meta-level judgment is itself an intellectual skill. Handing someone a checklist for "critical thinking" doesn't turn them into a critical thinker, just as handing someone a recipe doesn't make them a chef.

**Response (Integrate):** This is the strongest counterargument, and it genuinely modifies the thesis. The concession: mere possession of a procedure is insufficient. Engagement with the procedure — actually executing each step and exercising judgment within each step — matters. But this *still* supports the core claim. The modification is: thinking failures are procedural in the sense that the bottleneck is *having and following appropriate procedures*, not in the sense that procedures are a magic spell. A recipe doesn't make you a chef, but a chef without any recipes, mise en place, or methodology will produce worse food than the same chef with them. The skill is in the execution of procedure, not in the raw capacity to execute.

Furthermore, the evidence shows that even crude procedural interventions (simple checklists, "consider the opposite" prompts) produce large improvements in people who had no previous training. The threshold of "intellectual skill needed to follow a procedure" is far lower than the threshold of "intellectual skill needed to spontaneously generate the right cognitive moves."

### Counterargument 2: "You're ignoring genuine intellectual differences"

**Steelmanned version:** People differ in fluid intelligence, processing speed, working memory capacity, and acquired knowledge. These differences are real, heritable, and predictive of outcomes across many domains. To claim that thinking failures are "not intellectual" is to deny well-established individual differences research. A person with an IQ of 85 will not reason as well as a person with an IQ of 130, regardless of procedure.

**Response (Concede and contain):** This is factually correct and the thesis does not deny it. The key word in the thesis is "most." At the extremes of cognitive ability, intellectual capacity is genuinely the binding constraint. A person who cannot hold the relevant concepts in mind cannot be helped by a procedure that requires those concepts. But the thesis claims that *for the majority of thinking failures observed in the population of average-and-above-intelligence adults*, the limiting factor is not capacity but process. The evidence from Stanovich et al. supports this: across the normal-and-above IQ range, procedural factors (thinking dispositions, willingness to engage effortful processing) predict rational thinking outcomes far better than ability measures. Intelligence sets a floor; procedure determines how close to the ceiling you actually operate.

### Counterargument 3: "Some problems require genuine insight, not procedure"

**Steelmanned version:** History's great intellectual achievements — Darwin's theory of natural selection, Einstein's relativity, the invention of the transistor — were not the result of following procedures. They required creative leaps that no checklist could generate. Reducing thinking to procedure misses what makes human cognition most impressive and most valuable.

**Response (Concede and contain):** Granted. The thesis explicitly does not cover breakthrough creative insights. But this counterargument proves less than it appears to. First, genuine breakthrough insight is exceedingly rare even among brilliant people. The vast majority of thinking — even by scientists and inventors — is routine analysis, judgment, and decision-making where procedural failures dominate. Second, even creative work benefits enormously from procedural elements: systematic exploration of a problem space, disciplined testing of hypotheses, structured review of prior work. Darwin kept meticulous procedural notebooks. Third, the counterargument commits a base-rate fallacy: it points to the 0.1% of thinking tasks where insight is the bottleneck and uses them to argue against a claim about the other 99.9%.

### Counterargument 4: "This is just dressed-up behaviorism / it's trivially true"

**Steelmanned version:** Saying "people fail because they skip steps" is either trivially true (of course doing more thinking produces better results) or a repackaging of behaviorist stimulus-response thinking that ignores the rich internal cognitive processes that matter. Either way, it's not a useful insight.

**Response (Refute):** The claim is neither trivial nor behaviorist. It's not trivial because the dominant implicit theory — held by most people, most organizations, and most educational systems — is that thinking quality is primarily a function of intelligence and knowledge. Hiring practices select for IQ proxies. Educational institutions teach knowledge, not thinking procedures. When someone thinks poorly, the default attribution is "they're not smart enough" or "they don't know enough," not "they skipped a step." If the thesis were trivially accepted, we would see procedural thinking interventions everywhere. We don't.

It's not behaviorist because the thesis doesn't claim thinking is *nothing but* procedure. It claims the *failure mode* is primarily procedural. Rich internal cognitive processes exist and matter — the thesis says they fail to activate at the right times, not that they don't exist.

### Counterargument 5: "Motivation and emotion matter more than procedure"

**Steelmanned version:** People don't just skip steps randomly. They skip steps because they're anxious, overconfident, rushed, defensive, or motivated to reach a particular conclusion. The real cause of thinking failures is motivational and emotional, not procedural. Giving someone a procedure they don't want to follow accomplishes nothing.

**Response (Integrate):** This is an important and partially correct objection. Motivation is a genuine factor — the best procedure in the world fails if someone refuses to use it. However, this integrates with the thesis rather than defeating it. First, many procedural failures occur in people who are motivated and trying hard — they simply don't know which steps to take. A motivated person without a procedure is like a motivated person without a map: effort without direction. Second, well-designed procedures can *counteract* motivational biases by making certain steps obligatory. The surgical checklist works partly because it creates social pressure to follow steps that individuals might skip under time pressure. Third, even when motivation is the proximate cause of step-skipping, the counterfactual is illuminating: *if the procedure were in place and followed*, the emotional state would not have produced the error. The procedure is the protective layer against motivational contamination.

## Step 6: The Document

### Introduction

Why do smart people make bad decisions? Why do knowledgeable experts miss obvious flaws? Why do well-intentioned teams walk into foreseeable disasters?

The standard answer points to intellectual limitations: not smart enough, not informed enough, not creative enough. This answer is wrong — or at least, it is wrong for the vast majority of cases. The evidence from cognitive science, organizational research, and applied decision-making points to a different conclusion: **most thinking failures are procedural, not intellectual.** People fail not because they lack the cognitive capacity to think well, but because they skip steps, lose track of considerations, and lack structured processes that would activate the capabilities they already possess.

This matters because it changes the prescription. If thinking failures are intellectual, the solution is selection (hire smarter people) or education (teach more knowledge). If thinking failures are procedural, the solution is engineering — designing and deploying thinking procedures that prevent characteristic errors. One of these is tractable at scale. The other is not.

### Background

**Key definitions:**
- **Thinking failure:** A case where someone reaches a conclusion, makes a decision, or produces an analysis that is materially worse than what they were capable of, given their existing knowledge and cognitive ability.
- **Procedural failure:** A failure caused by not performing a cognitive step that the person was capable of performing. Omission, not incapacity.
- **Intellectual failure:** A failure caused by insufficient cognitive capacity — the person could not have done better even with optimal process.

**Scope:** This argument concerns adults of average or above-average intelligence performing everyday reasoning, professional judgment, and decision-making. It does not claim that clinical cognitive impairments are procedural, nor that breakthrough creative insights can be reduced to checklists.

**The implicit theory we're arguing against:** The default attribution for thinking failures in most cultures, organizations, and educational systems is intellectual. When someone makes a bad decision, the first inference is about their intelligence or knowledge, not about their process. This is reflected in hiring (IQ proxies), education (knowledge transmission), and organizational response to failures ("we need better people") rather than process design.

### Argument 1: The Error Pattern Is Procedural

If thinking failures were primarily intellectual, we would expect errors to be distributed according to task difficulty — harder problems would produce more failures, easier problems fewer, and the pattern would correlate with ability. Instead, what we observe is something quite different: errors cluster around *specific omissions* that are consistent regardless of problem difficulty or solver ability.

The heuristics-and-biases research tradition, spanning five decades, has catalogued dozens of characteristic errors. The critical observation is not that these errors exist but *how they are corrected.* In study after study, simply prompting people to perform a specific cognitive step — "consider the opposite," "think about the base rate," "generate an alternative explanation" — dramatically reduces error rates. The subjects do not need to become smarter. They need to be told which step to take.

Gary Klein's naturalistic decision-making research, studying experts in high-stakes environments, finds the same pattern from the opposite direction. Expert errors are overwhelmingly errors of situation assessment — failing to check a variable, failing to consider a scenario — not errors of reasoning once the situation is correctly assessed. The cognitive machinery works fine; the input procedure is incomplete.

Post-mortem analyses of organizational failures reinforce this. Finkelstein's study of spectacular corporate failures found, again and again, that decision-makers had the information, had the intelligence, and had the analytical frameworks. What they lacked was a process that would have forced them to confront disconfirming evidence, consider alternatives, or check their assumptions. The errors were sins of omission, not of commission.

### Argument 2: Intelligence Does Not Protect

If thinking failures were primarily intellectual, intelligence should be a strong protective factor. Smarter people should make fewer thinking errors, proportional to their advantage. The evidence says otherwise.

Keith Stanovich's research program on "dysrationalia" — irrational thinking in people with adequate intelligence — is the most systematic examination of this question. Across multiple studies using thousands of subjects, Stanovich and colleagues have shown that IQ correlates poorly with performance on rational thinking tasks. High-IQ individuals fall for the same biases: they neglect base rates, succumb to framing effects, exhibit myside bias, and fall prey to sunk-cost reasoning at rates that their intellectual superiority would not predict.

The finding is even more striking in some specific cases. West, Meserve, and Stanovich (2012) found that on certain bias tasks, higher cognitive ability was associated with *greater* bias — specifically, smarter people were better at constructing sophisticated justifications for conclusions they were motivated to reach. Intelligence was not protecting against the error; it was enabling a more polished version of it.

Philip Tetlock's forecasting research provides a real-world demonstration. Expert forecasters with high credentials and deep domain knowledge performed poorly when forecasting geopolitical events. Meanwhile, the "superforecasters" identified through the Good Judgment Project — who outperformed intelligence analysts with access to classified information — were distinguished not by extraordinary intelligence but by *procedural habits*: breaking problems into components, considering multiple perspectives, updating incrementally, tracking their own accuracy. Same species of task. Same general range of intelligence. Radically different process. Radically different outcomes.

### Argument 3: Adding Procedure Fixes the Problem

This is the most direct evidence for the thesis. If thinking failures were intellectual, adding procedure should not help much — you cannot checklist your way past a capacity limitation. If thinking failures are procedural, adding procedure should produce large improvements. The evidence overwhelmingly supports the latter.

Atul Gawande's documentation of surgical checklists provides a vivid case. A 19-item checklist, taking less than two minutes to complete, reduced surgical complications by 36% and deaths by 47% across eight hospitals in a WHO study. The surgeons did not attend new training. They did not become more knowledgeable. They followed a procedure that ensured critical steps were not skipped. The knowledge was always there; the activation mechanism was not.

In intelligence analysis, the adoption of Structured Analytic Techniques after the Iraq WMD failure demonstrated the same principle. Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, Key Assumptions Check, and similar procedures did not require analysts to be smarter. They required analysts to *systematically perform steps they were capable of but routinely skipped*: listing all hypotheses, evaluating evidence against each, identifying which evidence actually discriminates between hypotheses.

Daniel Kahneman's "mediating assessments protocol" for hiring — structuring interviews around independent evaluation of specific traits — reliably outperforms unstructured interviews conducted by experienced, intelligent evaluators. The unstructured interviewers are not dumber. They are procedurally unsupported, and as a result they fall prey to halo effects, confirmation bias, and affect-driven evaluation.

Gary Klein's pre-mortem technique provides perhaps the cleanest demonstration. Ask a team planning a project to "imagine it has failed spectacularly — now tell me why." This single procedural intervention consistently surfaces critical risks that the same team, moments before, had not identified through normal planning. No new information enters the room. No one becomes smarter. A procedural prompt activates thinking capacity that was present but dormant.

The pattern is consistent across all these domains: **same people + structured procedure = dramatically better outcomes.** This is the diagnostic signature of a procedural problem.

### Argument 4: We Recognize It in Hindsight

There is a subjective test for this thesis that nearly everyone can run. Think of your last significant thinking error — a bad decision, a missed risk, a flawed analysis. Now ask: "Was the problem that I *couldn't* have figured out the right answer, or that I *didn't*?"

For most people, most of the time, the answer is the latter. The characteristic feeling of thinking failure is not "that was beyond me" but "I should have thought of that." The knowledge was available. The reasoning capacity existed. What was missing was the step — the prompt, the check, the pause — that would have activated it.

This introspective evidence is admittedly weak on its own. But it is corroborated by the formal research. Fischhoff's work on hindsight bias acknowledges that while people overestimate the foreseeability of outcomes, there is a real kernel underneath: in many cases, the information and reasoning needed to foresee the outcome were genuinely available. James Reason's analysis of industrial accidents finds the same thing — the information needed to prevent disasters existed in the system, accessible to people with the intelligence to act on it. The missing ingredient was a process that would have brought the right information to the right attention at the right time.

### Argument 5: Cognitive Architecture Predicts This

The claim that most failures are procedural is not merely an empirical observation — it is predicted by what we know about human cognitive architecture.

Working memory, the mental workspace where active reasoning occurs, holds roughly four items simultaneously. Complex reasoning routinely requires tracking more considerations than this: the pros and cons of a decision, the evidence for and against a hypothesis, the assumptions behind a plan and the ways each could fail. Without external structure, items are dropped. This is not a failure of intelligence. It is a constraint of architecture, as universal as the bandwidth of a network connection.

Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory formalizes this: when the intrinsic demands of a task exceed working memory capacity, performance degrades. The solution is to reduce extraneous cognitive load — and one of the most effective ways to do this is to externalize the sequencing and tracking of steps. A procedure says: "You don't have to remember to check this. The checklist remembers for you."

Edwin Hutchins' work on distributed cognition shows that complex reasoning in real-world settings *always* relies on external cognitive structures — instruments, checklists, shared representations, social roles that distribute cognitive labor. The image of a brilliant individual reasoning entirely "in their head" is largely fiction. Even the most capable thinkers rely on paper, whiteboards, structured notes, and methodical processes. The question is not whether to externalize cognitive steps but whether to do so systematically or haphazardly.

### Counterarguments and Honest Engagement

**"Procedures require judgment to apply."** True. Merely possessing a checklist does not guarantee good thinking, just as possessing a recipe does not guarantee good cooking. The thesis does not claim that procedure is sufficient — it claims that procedure is the *binding constraint* for most failures. The intellectual threshold for following a well-designed procedure is far lower than the threshold for spontaneously generating the right cognitive moves without one.

**"Genuine intellectual differences exist."** Also true, and the thesis does not deny this. Intelligence sets a floor on what someone can reason about. But the evidence shows that across the normal-and-above range, procedure — not ability — determines how close people operate to their ceiling. The gap between people's potential and their actual performance is mostly procedural.

**"Breakthrough insights require more than procedure."** Conceded. The thesis covers the 99% of thinking that is routine analysis, judgment, and decision-making — not the 1% that is paradigm-breaking insight. Even within creative domains, procedural elements (systematic exploration, disciplined testing, structured review) play a larger role than the mythology of pure inspiration suggests.

**"Motivation and emotion are the real culprits."** Partially true, and this integrates with rather than defeats the thesis. Motivation determines whether someone follows a procedure. But procedure is the mechanism through which good thinking happens, and well-designed procedures can counteract motivational biases by making critical steps non-optional. The question "why did they skip the step?" (motivation) is different from "what would have prevented the failure?" (procedure).

### Synthesis

The five arguments converge on a single picture:

1. **The error pattern** is one of omission, not incapacity — people skip steps they could perform.
2. **Intelligence doesn't protect** — smarter people skip the same steps, sometimes more confidently.
3. **Adding procedure fixes it** — the same people, with structure, produce dramatically better results.
4. **People recognize it** — the phenomenology of failure is "I should have thought of that," not "I couldn't have figured that out."
5. **Architecture predicts it** — working memory limits make step-skipping the *expected* failure mode for any unsupported reasoning process.

Each argument is independently suggestive. Together, they form a mutually reinforcing case that is difficult to explain under the "thinking failures are intellectual" model but falls naturally out of the "thinking failures are procedural" model.

The modified thesis, strengthened by honest engagement with counterarguments: **For adults of average or above-average intelligence, across the vast majority of reasoning, judgment, and decision-making tasks, the binding constraint on thinking quality is not cognitive capacity but cognitive process. Most thinking failures are procedural — failures to execute steps the thinker was capable of — and are therefore addressable through better procedure rather than greater intelligence.**

### Conclusion

The case that thinking failures are primarily procedural carries a radical implication: **better thinking is an engineering problem, not a talent problem.** If most failures come from skipping steps, the intervention is to design systems that ensure steps are not skipped. This is achievable. It scales. It does not require finding smarter people or fundamentally reshaping human cognition.

This reframing shifts effort from selection to design. Rather than asking "who is smart enough to think well about this?" we should ask "what procedure would ensure the relevant considerations are activated?" Rather than attributing failure to inadequate intelligence, we should investigate which steps were skipped and why the process did not prevent it.

The evidence is clear: people are generally smart enough to think well. They need procedures that make their existing intelligence effective. The gap between how people think and how they could think is not a gap of capacity. It is a gap of process. And process gaps are closable.
