## SECTION D: Emotional Override Detection

### Step D1: Name the Emotion

The primary emotion is from the **Wanting** category: **intellectual excitement, flow-state satisfaction, the pleasure of building something elegant.**

Secondary emotion from the **Preserving** category: **attachment to the project as an identity artifact** — "I am the person building this novel thing."

Tertiary emotion from the **Avoiding** category: **avoidance of the discomfort of job searching, market validation, and the possibility of rejection.**

### Step D2: Intensity Scale

Rating: **7-8.** You feel urgency to keep building. Counter-arguments (like "get a job") produce agitation rather than calm consideration. The idea of stopping feels viscerally wrong, not just analytically suboptimal.

At 7+, your rational faculties are significantly impaired *for this specific decision.*

### Step D3: Functional Analysis

1. **What does this emotion want me to DO?** Keep building. Add more skills. Improve the website. Stay in the flow state. Avoid the market.

2. **What does this emotion want me to AVOID?** Job searching. Putting the product in front of people who might not care. The possibility that months of work don't translate to value for others. The experience of rejection.

3. **If I removed the emotion entirely and was a cold, calculating machine, what would I choose?** A cold machine would say: "You have a product. It might be good. You have zero data on whether anyone else wants it. The rational next step is not to build skill #593 — it is to spend the next 30 days exclusively on distribution and user acquisition. If, after 30 days of genuine effort, you have zero traction, you need to seriously consider whether this is a viable path. Continuing to build without any user contact is not product development — it is a hobby."

**The cold-machine answer differs from your emotional answer.** The emotion says "build more." The machine says "sell what you have or stop."

## The Honest Assessment

The question you asked — "Am I building this because it's genuinely useful or because building it is intellectually satisfying?" — is itself diagnostic. You already suspect the answer. The self-deception check confirms what you half-know:

**You are almost certainly building reasoningtool primarily because building it is intellectually satisfying.** This does not mean it isn't useful. It means the usefulness has not been established by anyone other than you, and the primary driver keeping you at the keyboard is the pleasure of the work itself.

### What this means (and doesn't mean):

1. **This does not mean reasoningtool is bad.** It might be genuinely valuable. You don't know yet because you haven't tested it.

2. **This does not mean you should stop.** It means you should stop pretending that continued building is the rational next step. The rational next step is user contact.

3. **The real self-deception is not "this is useful vs. this is fun."** The real self-deception is: **"Building more skills is progress."** It feels like progress. It has all the markers of progress — increasing numbers, visible output, daily accomplishments. But progress toward what? If the goal is "a product people use," then building without users is not progress. It is motion.

4. **The identity question is the deepest layer.** You are not just building a tool. You are being "someone who builds a novel, ambitious thing." That identity is threatened by market contact — because the market might not care, and then what are you? This is the thing you are protecting by not seeing it.

### The Three Real Reasons (the ones you'd be embarrassed to say aloud):

1. "I keep building because the flow state of designing thinking skills is one of the most enjoyable intellectual experiences I've ever had, and I don't want it to end."

2. "I keep building because as long as I'm building, I can tell myself I'm working on something important rather than admitting I'm unemployed and avoiding the job market."

3. "I keep building because putting it in front of real users might reveal that nobody wants this, and I'd rather live in the possibility than face the verdict."

## What Would Change Your Mind?

This is the final diagnostic. Complete this sentence honestly:

> "I would stop building reasoningtool if _______________."

- If you cannot name a specific, realistic condition: the decision to build is not evidence-based.
- If the condition you name is something that will never happen ("if thinking skills become illegal"): you are protecting the decision from reality.
- If the condition is testable and you haven't tested it: **test it. This week.**

A suggested testable condition: "I will spend the next 30 days with zero new skill development, focused entirely on getting 10 real people to use the existing 592 skills. If fewer than 3 out of 10 find them valuable enough to use again, I will seriously reassess."

The answer to your question is probably: **both — but the intellectual satisfaction is doing the steering, and the usefulness is the story you're telling about where you're going.**

The question is whether you're willing to let the market answer the question, or whether you'd rather keep building in the comfortable ambiguity of "it might be useful, I just need to find the right people."
