# /agsk Analyze the argument: "If thinking skills worked, smart people would already use them, and they don't"
**Date:** 2026-03-09
**Skill:** /agsk (Argument Analysis)

---

## Step 1: Identify the Claim

```
CLAIM: Thinking skills (structured reasoning procedures) don't work.
CLAIM TYPE: Empirical / causal
SCOPE: Universal — the argument implies thinking skills categorically fail to deliver value.
```

The argument uses the absence of adoption by smart people as evidence for ineffectiveness. The *real* assertion is about efficacy, with adoption behavior as the proof.

---

## Step 2: Identify the Premises

```
PREMISES:
  P1: If thinking skills worked, smart people would already use them.
      STATUS: Stated explicitly
  P2: Smart people don't use thinking skills.
      STATUS: Stated explicitly
  P3: Smart people are reliable detectors of what works for thinking.
      STATUS: Reconstructed — required for P1 to hold
  P4: "Working" means producing visibly better outcomes that smart people
      would notice and adopt.
      STATUS: Reconstructed — defines the implicit standard of evidence
```

---

## Step 3: Check Logical Validity

```
ARGUMENT STRUCTURE:
  IF [smart people reliably adopt what works for thinking (P3)]
  AND [smart people don't use thinking skills (P2)]
  THEN [thinking skills don't work (CLAIM)]
  VALID: Yes — the modus tollens form is logically valid
  FORM: Deductive (modus tollens)
```

The logical form is:
- If X works → smart people adopt X
- Smart people have not adopted X
- Therefore X does not work

This is valid modus tollens. The argument's problems are not in its logic but in the truth of its premises.

---

## Step 4: Check Premise Truth

### P1: If thinking skills worked, smart people would already use them.

```
P1 ASSESSMENT:
  TRUTH: False
  EVIDENCE FOR:
    - Markets are generally efficient; good tools tend to spread
    - Smart people actively seek advantage; they'd find effective tools
  EVIDENCE AGAINST:
    - Checklists demonstrably save lives in surgery, yet surgeon adoption
      took decades and institutional mandates (Gawande, "The Checklist Manifesto")
    - Cognitive behavioral therapy works but most people, including smart ones,
      never use structured thought records outside clinical settings
    - Pre-mortem analysis improves project planning (Klein, 1998) but most
      smart managers don't use it
    - Structured analytic techniques (SATs) improve intelligence analysis
      but were not widely adopted until mandated post-9/11
    - Bayesian reasoning demonstrably improves forecasting (Tetlock,
      "Superforecasting") yet most smart people don't use it explicitly
    - Smart people routinely fail to adopt proven practices: flossing,
      exercise, spaced repetition, journaling
  VERDICT: Weak — the history of effective-but-unadopted tools is long
```

### P2: Smart people don't use thinking skills.

```
P2 ASSESSMENT:
  TRUTH: Partially true
  EVIDENCE FOR:
    - Most people, including smart ones, don't use named/branded "thinking
      skills" or structured reasoning procedures
    - There is no mass-market product for structured thinking that has
      achieved broad adoption
  EVIDENCE AGAINST:
    - Many smart people DO use structured thinking procedures — they just
      don't call them "thinking skills": decision matrices, pros/cons lists,
      SWOT analysis, design thinking, scientific method, legal reasoning
      frameworks, diagnostic protocols
    - Consulting firms (McKinsey, BCG) are literally built on structured
      thinking frameworks applied systematically
    - The entire field of operations research is formalized thinking procedures
    - Chess players study structured decision procedures explicitly
    - Superforecasters use explicit calibration and decomposition procedures
  VERDICT: Weak — the premise relies on a narrow definition of "thinking skills"
           while ignoring that structured reasoning is widespread under other names
```

### P3 (Reconstructed): Smart people are reliable detectors of what works for thinking.

```
P3 ASSESSMENT:
  TRUTH: False
  EVIDENCE FOR:
    - Smart people are generally better at evaluating tools and methods
    - Intelligence correlates with ability to assess what works
  EVIDENCE AGAINST:
    - Smart people are demonstrably bad at meta-cognition about their own
      thinking (the bias blind spot — Pronin et al., 2002)
    - Intelligence actually INCREASES confidence in one's own unaided
      reasoning, reducing motivation to adopt tools (Stanovich, "dysrationalia")
    - Smart people suffer from "earned dogmatism" — past success makes them
      trust their intuition more, not less
    - The Dunning-Kruger effect's lesser-known flip side: competent people
      assume what's easy for them is easy for everyone, so they don't see
      the value in systematizing it
    - Expertise often makes people WORSE at recognizing when they need
      structure (expert overconfidence is well-documented in forecasting)
  VERDICT: Weak — intelligence and good meta-cognitive tool selection are
           different capacities
```

### P4 (Reconstructed): "Working" means producing visibly better outcomes that smart people would notice and adopt.

```
P4 ASSESSMENT:
  TRUTH: Partially true
  EVIDENCE FOR:
    - If something works dramatically, the effect should be visible
  EVIDENCE AGAINST:
    - Many effective interventions have subtle, cumulative, or
      counterfactual benefits that are hard to observe (avoiding errors
      you never made, slightly better average decisions over time)
    - Thinking quality is notoriously hard to measure — unlike physical
      tools, you can't easily A/B test your own reasoning
    - Selection effects obscure the signal: people who think well may
      attribute success to intelligence rather than their methods
  VERDICT: Weak — the standard of evidence is unreasonably high for a
           domain where measurement is inherently difficult
```

## Step 5: Identify Hidden Assumptions

```
HIDDEN ASSUMPTIONS:
  A1: The adoption curve for thinking tools should match the adoption curve
      for physical tools or technology.
      PLAUSIBILITY: Low
      IF FALSE: Thinking tools may work but face unique adoption barriers
      (invisibility of benefit, ego threat, no feedback loop) that don't
      apply to physical tools. The absence of adoption proves nothing about
      efficacy.

  A2: Smart people's current thinking is already near-optimal, so there's
      little room for improvement.
      PLAUSIBILITY: Low
      IF FALSE: Even smart people make systematic reasoning errors (this
      is the entire finding of the heuristics and biases research program).
      The room for improvement is large even at the top.

  A3: If structured thinking worked, it would be discovered and adopted
      organically without deliberate tooling or education.
      PLAUSIBILITY: Low
      IF FALSE: Many effective practices require deliberate instruction
      and institutional support. The scientific method itself had to be
      invented and taught — it wasn't obvious to even the smartest ancient
      thinkers.

  A4: "Smart people" is a coherent reference class whose behavior can be
      observed and generalized from.
      PLAUSIBILITY: Medium
      IF FALSE: Different smart people in different domains DO use structured
      reasoning — the argument cherry-picks the ones who don't.

  A5: Individual adoption is the right metric, rather than institutional
      adoption.
      PLAUSIBILITY: Medium
      IF FALSE: Institutions DO adopt structured thinking procedures
      extensively (aviation checklists, medical protocols, legal procedures,
      engineering review processes). The argument only works if you look at
      individual informal reasoning.
```

---

## Step 6: Assess Strength of Evidence

```
EVIDENCE ASSESSMENT:
  TOTAL EVIDENCE CITED: 0 (the argument cites no evidence — it relies
  entirely on an assumed observation about smart people's behavior)
  TYPES: Anecdotal / assumed observational
  STRONGEST PIECE: The general observation that smart people don't use
    branded "thinking skills products" — this is roughly true as a market
    observation
  WEAKEST PIECE: The implicit claim that smart people don't use ANY
    structured thinking — this is straightforwardly false
  MISSING EVIDENCE:
    - Actual studies on the efficacy of structured reasoning procedures
      (which exist and generally show positive effects)
    - Adoption data for specific structured thinking methods in specific
      populations
    - Evidence on WHY smart people do or don't adopt reasoning tools
      (motivation, awareness, ego, friction)
  OVERALL EVIDENCE QUALITY: Absent
```

---

## Step 7: Find the Strongest Objection

```
STRONGEST OBJECTION:
  OBJECTION: Smart people DO use structured thinking procedures — extensively —
  they just don't use the branded term "thinking skills." The entire argument
  rests on a definitional sleight of hand. Consulting frameworks, diagnostic
  protocols, legal reasoning structures, scientific methodology, engineering
  review checklists, and decision analysis are all "thinking skills" that
  smart people not only use but get paid specifically to apply. The argument
  confuses "smart people don't use THIS PARTICULAR product" with "smart
  people don't use structured thinking," which is false.

  TARGETS: P2 (smart people don't use thinking skills) — attacks both the
  premise's truth and its definition of terms

  FORCE: Devastating

  POSSIBLE RESPONSE: The arguer might say "I mean structured procedures for
  general everyday thinking, not domain-specific professional procedures."

  RESPONSE QUALITY: Weak — this retreat concedes the core point. If structured
  procedures work in professional domains (which they demonstrably do), the
  question becomes "can they be generalized?" rather than "do they work?"
  And the answer to "can they be generalized?" is an empirical question
  the argument never addresses.
```

---

## Step 8: Overall Assessment

```
ARGUMENT QUALITY
================
CLAIM: Thinking skills don't work (as evidenced by non-adoption by smart people).
VALIDITY: Valid — the modus tollens form is logically correct
SOUNDNESS: Unsound — multiple premises are false or unsupported
EVIDENCE: Absent — the argument cites no evidence and relies on a false observation
STRONGEST POINT: There IS a real adoption puzzle worth explaining — if structured
  thinking tools are so effective, why isn't there a massively successful consumer
  product for them? This is a legitimate question about market dynamics.
WEAKEST POINT: The premise that smart people don't use structured thinking is
  straightforwardly false. They do, constantly, in professional contexts. What
  they don't do is use branded general-purpose reasoning tools in informal
  settings — but that's a distribution/packaging problem, not an efficacy problem.
OVERALL GRADE: Weak
VERDICT: Reject. The argument is logically valid but factually unsound. It
  confuses an adoption/distribution problem with an efficacy problem, ignores
  extensive evidence of structured thinking working in professional domains,
  and relies on a hidden assumption (that smart people are reliable self-directed
  adopters of meta-cognitive tools) that is contradicted by research on expert
  overconfidence and the bias blind spot.
```

---

## What the Argument Actually Reveals

The argument, while unsound, points to three real challenges that are worth taking seriously:

1. **The packaging problem.** Structured thinking works in institutions (aviation, medicine, consulting) where it's mandated and embedded in workflow. It hasn't been successfully packaged for individual voluntary use at scale. This is a real product challenge.

2. **The ego barrier.** Smart people resist thinking tools because using one implies their unaided thinking isn't good enough. This is a real adoption barrier that the argument itself demonstrates — it's essentially saying "I'm smart enough not to need this."

3. **The measurement problem.** Thinking quality improvements are genuinely hard to observe, making it rational to be skeptical of claimed benefits. Unlike a faster computer or a sharper knife, better thinking doesn't announce itself.

The strongest version of this objection isn't "thinking skills don't work" — it's "thinking skills work but face adoption barriers so severe that they may never achieve individual-use product-market fit." That's a much harder argument to dismiss, and it's the one worth engaging with.
