# /rci Keep asking "why" about: "People make bad decisions." How deep does the causal chain go?
**Date:** 2026-03-09
**Skill:** /rci (Recursive Causal Interrogation)

---

"People make bad decisions."

Why did I accept that as a starting point? It feels obvious. That's suspicious. What's compressed inside it?

Three words doing heavy lifting: "people," "bad," and "decisions."

Start with "bad." Bad relative to what? To their own goals? To outcomes they later regret? To some external standard of rationality? These are different claims. Which one is meant?

If bad means "producing outcomes they didn't want" -- is that true? Do people reliably produce outcomes they didn't want? Sometimes. But sometimes people get exactly what they wanted and it turns out they wanted the wrong thing. Is that a bad decision or a bad want? Those are different problems.

Wait -- what caused me to distinguish those? Because if the problem is bad wants, then improving decision-making doesn't help. You'd make better decisions toward goals that are themselves wrong. Is that what's happening? Is the claim actually "people want the wrong things"?

No -- back up. Is it even true that people make bad decisions? Compared to what? Compared to perfect rationality? Everything is bad compared to perfect rationality. Compared to what they could feasibly do? That's the real question. Could they feasibly do better?

Why couldn't they? What's stopping them?

**Branch 1: They don't have enough information.**

Why don't they have enough information? Either the information isn't available, or it is available and they don't access it, or they access it and can't process it.

If it's available and they don't access it -- why not? They don't know it exists? They don't know it's relevant? They're too busy? They assume they already know enough?

That last one. "Assume they already know enough." Why would someone assume that? Because the situation *feels* familiar. Pattern matching fires. The brain says "I've seen this before" and stops seeking. Is that a decision? They didn't decide to stop seeking information. The seeking just... stopped. A process that should have continued didn't. Why?

Because continuing costs energy. The brain is an energy miser. Thinking is metabolically expensive. So there's a physical constraint -- literal glucose, literal ATP -- that pushes toward premature closure. Is that the root? A thermodynamic constraint on cognition?

Maybe. But other animals also have energy constraints and they don't "make decisions" in the same sense. What's different about humans? Humans operate in environments far more complex than the ones their cognitive architecture evolved for. The pattern-matching that worked on the savanna misfires in a world of mortgages, career choices, and global supply chains. Is that the root? Evolutionary mismatch?

Question that. Is it actually mismatch? Or is the architecture working fine and the problem is something else? The architecture produces fast, low-energy assessments. In the ancestral environment, a wrong assessment might mean you ran from a shadow -- low cost. In the modern environment, a wrong assessment might mean you took on debt you can't repay -- high cost. Same architecture, different cost structure. So the problem isn't that the architecture is broken. The problem is that the *consequences* of its normal operation have changed.

But that's a forward-facing observation (consequences). Go back. What caused the architecture to be this way?

Evolution optimized for reproductive fitness, not for decision quality. These overlap sometimes and diverge other times. There was never selection pressure for "making good decisions" as such. There was selection pressure for surviving and reproducing. Decisions that were reproductively successful but objectively terrible (in-group bias, status-seeking, short-term thinking) got baked in because they worked *for that purpose*.

Is that the root? Evolution didn't select for good decisions?

That's a cause but is it *the* cause? It explains the hardware. But humans also have culture, education, tools. These can compensate for hardware limitations. Why don't they compensate enough?

**Branch 2: The tools exist but people don't use them.**

Why don't people use thinking tools? They don't know about them? They know but don't use them? They use them but use them wrong?

Most people have never been explicitly taught how to make a decision. School teaches content, not process. You learn history but not how to evaluate evidence. You learn math but not how to reason under uncertainty about your own life. Why?

Because the education system wasn't designed to produce good decision-makers. It was designed to produce literate workers. That's a historical cause -- the Prussian model, industrial-era needs. But that was centuries ago. Why hasn't it changed?

Because the people who would change it are products of the same system. They don't see the gap because they were never taught to see gaps in their own cognition. This is a loop. The system produces people who can't see the system's flaws, so the system perpetuates.

Is that really true? Some people do see the gaps. Researchers, educators, some practitioners. Why doesn't their insight propagate?

Because knowing something and implementing it at scale are different problems. Implementing requires institutional change. Institutional change requires overcoming inertia, incentives, and politics. The people who control institutions are optimizing for institutional survival, not for decision-quality of the population.

But why? Because good decisions by the population aren't what institutions are rewarded for. Institutions are rewarded for their own metrics. A school is rewarded for graduation rates, not for the decision-quality of graduates twenty years later. The feedback loop is too slow and too diffuse. No one is accountable for decision quality.

**Branch 3: People can't see their own thinking.**

Even when someone has information and tools -- why might they still decide badly? Because they can't observe their own reasoning process. You don't watch yourself think. You experience the output -- a preference, a feeling, a conclusion -- but not the process that generated it. You can't debug what you can't see.

Why can't you see it? Because most cognition is subconscious. The conscious mind receives results, not workings. This isn't a bug -- it's how the system works. Consciousness is a narrow spotlight on a vast processing system. You get the summary, not the spreadsheet.

So people are making decisions based on summaries of processes they can't inspect. Is that the core problem?

Question that. Even if you could inspect the process, would you know what to look for? You'd need a model of what good reasoning looks like to spot where yours went wrong. Most people don't have that model. And the people who do have that model (trained rationalists, decision scientists) -- do they make reliably better decisions?

The evidence is mixed. Knowing about biases doesn't reliably debias you. Awareness of cognitive errors doesn't prevent them. Why not?

Because the errors aren't happening at the level of awareness. They're happening upstream -- in how attention is allocated, how frames are constructed, how options are generated. By the time you're "deciding," the real work is already done. The decision is largely determined by which options you're considering, and that was shaped by processes you didn't choose and can't directly control.

**Branch 4: The frame precedes the decision.**

This is deeper. People don't make bad decisions. People make reasonable decisions *within frames they didn't choose*. The frame determines the decision. If you're choosing between A and B, and the real answer is C, no amount of careful deliberation between A and B helps. The error happened when the option space was constructed, not when the choice was made.

What constructs the frame? Culture, experience, language, recent context, emotional state, identity, social environment. None of these are chosen. You're born into a language, a culture, a family, a socioeconomic position. These construct the frames you'll use for the rest of your life, unless something disrupts them.

What disrupts frames? Crisis. Exposure to radically different perspectives. Certain kinds of education. Psychedelics. Meditation. Therapy. Intense suffering. Travel. Loss. These all work by the same mechanism: they break the frame, and in the gap, you can see that you were inside one.

But most people most of the time are inside their frames and can't see out. This isn't a failure. It's how cognition works. Frames are necessary. You can't process raw reality. You need compression. You need categories, assumptions, defaults. The frame is the compression. And compression loses information.

**Convergence point.**

The branches are converging. Let me trace what's emerging:

1. Evolution didn't optimize for decision quality -- it optimized for reproductive fitness, leaving hardware that misfires in modern contexts.
2. The conscious mind receives outputs, not processes -- you can't inspect your own reasoning.
3. Frames precede decisions -- the option space is constructed by processes you don't control and mostly can't see.
4. Education and culture don't compensate -- they perpetuate the gap through a self-reinforcing loop.
5. Even knowing about the problem doesn't fix it -- awareness operates at a different level than where the errors occur.

So what's underneath all of these?

**Cognition is compression. Compression loses information. Decisions are made on compressed representations. The information that was lost in compression is sometimes the information that would have changed the decision.**

That's the structural root. Not a moral failing, not stupidity, not laziness. A feature of how information processing works in finite systems. Any system that must act in a complex world with limited processing capacity will compress, and compression will sometimes lose what matters.

But wait -- question that. Is that really the deepest level? Why must the system compress? Because it has finite processing capacity. Why is it finite? Because it's physical. Brains are physical objects with physical limits. That's a brute fact. Thermodynamics. You can't process infinite information with finite matter and energy.

Is *that* the root? The physical finitude of the brain?

Almost. But there's one more step. The problem isn't just compression. It's *invisible* compression. If you knew what you were losing, you could compensate. The problem is that the compression is transparent -- you don't see it happening. The frame feels like reality, not like a frame. The summary feels like the whole picture. The map feels like the territory.

**The root: Cognitive compression is invisible to the system performing it. You don't experience your understanding as compressed -- you experience it as complete. The feeling of "I understand this" is not a measurement of understanding. It's a generated signal that stops further inquiry. And that signal fires too early, too often, because firing it is cheap and continuing to inquire is expensive.**

The "bad decisions" are a downstream symptom. The actual cause is a system that generates premature completeness signals -- that tells itself "you know enough" before it does -- because the cost of continued inquiry exceeds the expected benefit *as estimated by the same system that hasn't inquired enough*.

The system that estimates "is it worth thinking more?" is using the same compressed representation that would be improved by thinking more. It's asking a map whether the map is detailed enough. The map always says yes.

---

**Loop detected.** This is now self-referential: the process of questioning "people make bad decisions" is itself subject to the same premature-completeness dynamics it's describing. I stopped where I stopped for the same reasons the chain identifies. Have I gone deep enough? The system says yes. But the system always says yes.

**Brute fact reached.** At the bottom: physical finitude produces compression, compression is self-invisible, and self-invisible compression generates false completeness signals. This is structural, not fixable by willpower or knowledge alone. It's addressable only by systems that *externalize* the questioning -- that don't rely on the same compressed representation to evaluate whether the compressed representation is sufficient.

Which is what this skill does. Which is what the reasoning toolkit is. The tool exists because the problem exists. The problem is the reason for the tool. That's the connection to the context: reasoningtool exists because cognitive compression is invisible, and the only countermeasure is a process that forces re-entry into spaces that felt complete.
